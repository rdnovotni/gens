# Politics and Society

Rome is not conquered with a sword alone. A gens rises or falls on who owes it favors, who fears it, who speaks well of it at the baths, and who can be quietly ruined before anyone notices you did it. This chapter covers the tools of standing, organization, foreign relations, leverage, law, governance, and correspondence that let you play Roman society the way you'd play a battlefield — with patience, nerve, and a clear sense of who actually holds the knife.

## Patrons, Clients, and Factions

At the center of your household's public life sits **Dignitas** — a single tracked number that is, in effect, your household's reputation score. Nearly everything you do moves it: how grand your villa looks, the monuments you raise, the games you fund, the marriages you make, the battles you win, the office you hold, the scandal that breaks over your name, the debt you default on. Other systems read Dignitas constantly — it colors how a magistrate rules your court case, how eagerly a family will marry into yours, how a rival gens sizes you up. Treat it as your household's most important slow-moving asset. Full mechanical detail lives in [Politics & Patronage](../design/gens-politics-patronage-design.md).

On a frontier or a newly-annexed province, Dignitas actually splits into two separate tracks: your **Standing with Rome** and your **local standing** with the people around you. These can diverge sharply and stay diverged — a governor beloved in the Senate and despised by the provincials he rules is a real, playable, and often uncomfortable position. Winning a magistracy can raise one track while quietly costing you the other.

### Clientela — patrons and clients

**Clientela** is the real, working patron-client relationship at the heart of Roman social life, and in Gens it's a genuine roster you build and maintain, not a flavor label. Your clients each carry a **Specialty** — Legal, Mercantile, Martial, Religious, or Administrative — and each specialty implies a different favor they can do for you: courtroom testimony, a trade tip, muscle for a dispute, a friendly ear on the Curia. Calling in favors too often without giving anything back sours the relationship; Clientela is reciprocal, not a free tap.

The morning **Salutatio** — clients calling on you in your own Atrium — is a recurring event in its own right. A large, well-regarded clientele generates a small trickle of Dignitas and **Influence** just by being seen holding court well; a thin or resentful one costs you instead. Influence is the currency of political action — spent on backing an election, attempting a scheme, or throwing weight behind a favor at scale — and, like Loyalty, it decays if left unused. Growing your roster happens through travel encounters, events, direct recruitment, or offering clientage as a kinder alternative to debt bondage.

Watch your clients' **Loyalty** and **Ambition**. A client whose favors go unrewarded, or who's been offered a better deal by a rival house, is a real poaching risk — the bond can flip entirely. The same dynamic runs in reverse: courting a rival's discontented client is a legitimate weapon of your own.

### Office and the cursus honorum

Local office is the achievable political ladder every household can climb inside a single playthrough. Once your settlement has a Curia, you can contest a seat as a **Decurion**, then climb toward **Aedile** (funding public works and games), **Quaestor** (which is what actually unlocks setting your own tax rates), and finally **Duumvir**, the paired chief-magistracy that unlocks the local Mint. Every office runs on an annual term, and can be lost early to insolvency or a court conviction, not just to losing re-election. Contested elections pit you against a real, named rival, weighing your attributes, Dignitas, and spent Influence against theirs.

The Curia itself is a real body of named councilors, each with their own Faction leaning and their own opinion of you — courting them ahead of a vote matters.

Far above all of this sits the **cursus honorum** — the traditional Senate track (Quaestor, Aedile or Tribune, Praetor, Consul), reachable only at Rome, not your municipality. This is deliberately a distant, rare, mostly-abstracted goal rather than a system you work through session to session: you need to be *noticed*, which means a strong Dignitas-with-Rome, ideally a sponsor, and enough real wealth to clear a genuine property census. A modest household that claws its way to the Senate is playing out the celebrated *novus homo* story — Rome's own rags-to-riches arc — and it's rare enough that reaching it is real history for your dynasty. A provincial governorship is a related, separate prize reachable through the same channel, and it's what eventually opens tax-farming contracts to your household.

As a sitting magistrate, you also gain the power to issue a **Sumptuary Edict** — a standing restriction on who may publicly display certain luxuries. Enforcing one wins favor with traditional-minded onlookers and irritates the wealthier merchant class you're actually restricting; it also makes a useful, if petty, weapon against a rival flaunting new money.

### Faction

Every politically relevant character, including your own household, carries a light **Faction** leaning: **Traditionalist** (conservative, ancestral-custom-favoring) or **Popularist** (reform- and common-favor-leaning). This is not a hard allegiance or an organization you join — it's a single tag that quietly shapes how people react to your sumptuary enforcement, how susceptible someone is to a scheme against them, and where the natural friction sits among your fellow Curia members. Your own household's Faction lean shifts slowly, in response to the choices you actually make, rather than being picked once and forgotten.

Faction is easy to mistake for one of the two organizing structures below — it isn't either of them. It's a permanent belief label, not a group you can recruit into or a temporary cause you rally around. Those are genuinely different things, covered next.

## Organizations You Can Join or Build

Faction tells you what a household broadly believes. Two further structures answer different questions entirely, and it's worth keeping all three straight, because Gens treats them as three deliberately distinct things rather than variations on one idea.

### Collegia & Guilds — permanent, trade-based

A **collegium** is Rome's real historical answer to organizing everyone the Curia and cursus honorum shut out — Peregrines, freedmen, ordinary tradespeople. It's a genuine, structured organization with officers (a **Magister** at the head, a **Quinquennalis** overseeing membership and finances), dues flowing into a shared treasury (the **Arca**), and often a meeting hall (a **Schola**) it can own outright. Four real kinds coexist: trade guilds (potters, weavers, shippers), burial societies that guarantee members a decent funeral regardless of their wealth, cult-specific collegia built around a shared foreign or minor deity, and neighborhood associations centered on a local crossroads shrine.

Collegia are permanent institutions, not passing coalitions. Sponsoring one as a patron is one of the most efficient political moves available to you — a single relationship buys you an entire grateful bloc of clients at once, rather than the one-by-one grind of ordinary Clientela recruitment, and the collegium displays your name with real pride. A collegium's organized endorsement is also a genuine asset — or liability — in a contested election.

There's a darker edge worth knowing about: organized collegium membership has real historical precedent for intimidation and outright political violence, especially in the late Republic. Using a collegium this way is a serious, high-risk exercise of raw power, and Rome itself treated collegia with real suspicion for exactly this reason — a collegium can be formally **Licitum** (authorized, safe) or **Illicit** (unauthorized or dissolved for cause), and a magistrate can shut an Illicit one down, with real reputational fallout for any patron publicly tied to it.

For a Freedman or Peregrine shut out of formal office entirely, rising to Magister or Quinquennalis is a genuine, respected achievement in its own right — a second prestige ladder, not a lesser one. See [Collegia & Guilds](../design/gens-collegia-guilds-design.md) for the full picture.

### Interest Groups — temporary, cross-cutting coalitions

An **Interest Group** answers a question neither Faction nor Collegia can: who actually stands to gain or lose from *this specific policy*, regardless of ideology or trade? A Traditionalist old landowner and a newly-wealthy Popularist one can both oppose a Land Redistribution Edict for entirely different reasons — shared material interest, not shared belief, is what pulls them together. Five real coalitions exist: Landowners vs. the Landless, Creditors vs. Debtors, Publicani and Equestrian trade interests, Veterans, and Provincial interests tied to your household's own home region.

Most Interest Groups are genuinely temporary — they form the moment a relevant policy question goes live (a proposed Edict, a contested election with a clear stake) and dissolve once it resolves. A couple, like the Publicani/Equestrian bloc and the Veterans, are effectively permanent standing coalitions, since their underlying stake never fully settles. Membership isn't something you opt into — it's read automatically from your household's actual circumstances (your debts, your land, your veteran status, your home region), and you can belong to several groups whose material interests contradict your own household's Faction lean without any tension in the system itself.

The richest piece of this system is **Provincial Patronage** — a real Roman institution in which a province formally designates a senator as its own **patronus** in the Senate. Holding this role is a genuine, higher-stakes cousin of ordinary Clientela: real Dignitas and standing influence over policy touching that region, but a real obligation to actually defend it, with a serious cost if you don't. It's also one of the few direct levers you have over a distant region's own local-standing trajectory. See [Interest Groups](../design/gens-interest-groups-design.md).

## Beyond Rome's Borders

### Client kingdoms and vassal rulers

Not every neighboring throne needs to become a province outright. A **Client Kingdom** relationship runs on its own track, entirely separate from Reputation Duality — call it **Tribute & Fealty**, moving between Loyal Client, Reluctant Client, Defiant, and Broken Away. Left unattended, it drifts downward on its own, like any other standing relationship in this game.

**Investiture** is how Rome makes a king: a formal recognition of a new or disputed ruler, sometimes a personal ceremony at Rome itself, more often a simpler act delivered through a Legate or governor. Whoever sponsors an Investiture earns real standing for it, and the newly-crowned ruler starts their reign owing that sponsor a personal debt of gratitude on top of the kingdom's baseline relationship with Rome.

The relationship runs on real, recurring content: **tribute** (a standing income stream, with rate a genuine choice — generous sustains loyalty, harsh extracts more now at real risk later) and **auxiliary levies** (real troops supplied for a Roman campaign, often in exchange for eventual citizenship for those who serve). **Obsides** — hostages, typically a ruler's own children — are a distinctly Roman practice: not prisoners, but a mutual guarantee, very often raised inside a Roman household and absorbing Roman culture before returning home to rule. A hostage who comes home genuinely Assimilated makes a far more pliable client ruler; one who comes home Resentful makes an unusually dangerous one. This is a genuine multi-generation gamble, not a guaranteed payoff.

When a ruler dies, is deposed, or a returning hostage presses a claim, a **Succession Crisis** fires, and you can back a preferred claimant through Investiture, diplomacy, or outright military support. Where a second Great Power also borders the kingdom, the same crisis escalates into a real contest between outside patrons rather than a purely domestic affair.

A client relationship can end three ways. **Conversion to Province** — the historically common endpoint — happens when a ruler dies heirless, Rome simply decides to absorb the kingdom, or a crushed rebellion ends in annexation; the former ruling house is either folded into the new provincial elite or meets a harsher fate. **Breaking Away** is the opposite: a client's relationship collapsing all the way into open rebellion, resolved as a genuine war against a real royal army, not a mere uprising. Or the relationship simply holds, indefinitely, exactly as the Bosporan Kingdom's did for centuries. Full depth is in [Client Kingdoms & Vassal Rulers](../design/gens-client-kingdoms-vassal-rulers-design.md).

### Diplomacy with non-Roman peoples

Ordinary frontier tribes and genuine Great Powers get treated very differently, and Gens keeps that distinction honest. Every negotiation with a foreign people or a Parthian counterpart reads an **Interpreter Quality** input: with no qualified person present, you negotiate at a real penalty; with a Character whose own culture matches or has drifted toward the target people's, you negotiate at full strength for free; a hired interpreter closes most, but not all, of that gap — a direct payoff for any cultural investment made elsewhere.

Your standing action is a **Frontier Relations Posture** — Guarded Distance, Active Engagement, or Subjugation Pressure — and your diplomatic toolkit runs from a Treaty of Non-Aggression and Tribute Arrangements through Marriage Alliances, Foederati Pacts, and a lighter-weight **Diplomatic Gift**. **Frontier Hostage-Taking** is worth distinguishing from Parthia's own practice: demanding hostages from a tribe as a condition of peace is coercive and one-directional; Parthia's own **Hostage Exchange** is a genuinely mutual arrangement between peers. Diplomacy fails sometimes, too — a hostile or feuding people generates real raids against your frontier holdings, answerable with a **Retaliatory Campaign** aimed at restoring standing rather than conquest.

At the far end of Roman-Parthian relations sits **Alliance Against Rome** — the point of no return for a household that's reached a genuine breaking point with Rome itself. It resolves through three stages (Secret Negotiation, Open Declaration, the war itself) and, if you win, in real gradations: a **Negotiated Autonomy**, the more common and historically honest outcome, or a rare, dramatic **Full Clean Break** where your household becomes the founding dynasty of something entirely new. At the furthest extreme, you can seek direct Parthian backing — far more effective, and far riskier to keep secret. None of this is exclusive to you: rival houses can be pursuing their own alliance against Rome entirely independently, and uncovering a rival's secret treason through espionage is potent leverage in its own right. See [Diplomacy with Non-Roman Peoples](../design/gens-diplomacy-non-roman-peoples-design.md).

## Scandal and Secrets

Roman political life runs as much on what people know about each other as on what they say in public. Three systems govern this territory, and they hand off to each other in a clean sequence: a **Secret** exists, someone acquires **leverage** over it, and if it ever goes public, **Scandal** takes over the aftermath.

### Secrets & Hooks

A **Secret** is a real, dated fact about a specific character — an affair, a hidden illegitimate child, a buried crime, a concealed debt, a proscribed cult, treason — that exists the moment the underlying event happens, whether or not anyone currently knows about it. It carries a severity tier (from a minor embarrassment up to something worthy of formal Senate censure) that describes exactly how bad it would read if it ever became public.

A Secret becomes known through active investigation (a spy or an intrigue-driven scheme), simply being witnessed by someone present, a confession, or intercepted correspondence. Whoever learns it holds a **Hook** — knowledge with actual leverage attached, Strong or Weak depending on how solid the discovery was. A Hook can be used to **Compel** a specific action (a vote, breaking a betrothal, stepping down from a position), to **Extort** an ongoing drain of money or influence, or to **Expose** the secret outright, which trades away your leverage for pure, public damage to the target. A Hook can also be gifted or sold to an ally, and it can be faked entirely — a **Fabricated Hook** that behaves exactly like a real one until someone seriously challenges it, at which point discovery backfires hard on whoever fabricated it. If you'd rather get ahead of the whole business, a **Preemptive Confession** costs you real standing but at a real discount, and it destroys every Hook anyone else was holding over you. Full detail: [Secrets & Hooks](../design/gens-secrets-hooks-design.md).

### Scandal

**Scandal** owns everything that happens once a Secret — or an unjust execution, a disgraced performance, a weaponized lawsuit — actually goes public. It's tracked as a real record with two independent dimensions: **Severity** (how bad the matter reads) and **Scope** (how far it's actually spread — household-only, settlement-wide, provincial, or Rome-wide). Spread itself moves through channels you already know — ambient gossip among the local population, or a slower, real transit delay if it's carried by letter to a distant audience — and can be deliberately accelerated by an anonymous defamatory pamphlet (a real Roman practice, the *libellus famosus*) or a targeted rumor.

Consequences scale with severity and scope: a Dignitas penalty, a relationship scar, and for a severe or public case, a lasting **Scandal-Marked** trait. Audiences read the same scandal differently by Faction. And there's a genuine paradox worth knowing: for someone already famous *and* disreputable — an actor, a gladiator, a courtesan — a fresh scandal can actually raise their fame even as it further damages their standing. The rare, most severe cases can trigger **Nota Censoria** — a formal Senate censure, entirely independent of any criminal conviction.

You're not purely at the mercy of events once a scandal breaks. **Damage control** gives you real options: **Suppression** (spending influence or bribes to cap the spread before it reaches wider scope), **Spin** (a funded public gesture that competes for the crowd's attention), the genuinely dark option of **Scapegoating** a dependent or enslaved member of your household rather than taking the blame yourself, and **Rehabilitation** — sustained good conduct that eventually earns back a lost reputation. See [Scandal](../design/gens-scandal-design.md).

### Espionage

**Espionage** is where you actually run a spy network — it names the kind of compromising material a spy might dig up, but the Secret itself and the leverage it produces belong to the system above. You can run a cheap, one-shot **Quick Op** against a single target, or invest in a **Persistent Network** — an embedded agent inside a rival house, the local administration, or any other faction of note, delivering blackmail material, early warning of schemes against you, and covert sabotage for as long as the placement survives. A persistent placement isn't free or riskless: it costs upkeep, your spymaster can only run so many at once, and discovery risk climbs the longer it stays in place.

If your spy is caught, what happens next isn't fixed. They might simply be eliminated, ransomed, or — more dangerous for you — **turned** into a double agent feeding you convincing disinformation without your knowledge. And your own spy's Loyalty is a live risk independent of anything the target does: a long-embedded agent can simply defect on their own initiative if their loyalty erodes. See [Espionage](../design/gens-espionage-design.md).

### Kidnap & Ransom — where it all comes together

Several stray threads across this space — a bare "kidnap" stub, the crime system's ransom machinery (built originally for a battlefield captive, not a targeted snatch), and banditry's own contract-kidnapping paths — finally connect in the dedicated **Kidnap & Ransom** mechanic: a real scheme for planning and executing a targeted kidnapping, negotiating a ransom that can include more than gold (a Hook handed over, a debt forgiven, a forced betrothal broken), keeping a hostage as a standing threat rather than cashing out immediately, and mounting an actual rescue. The piracy and banditry side of where a kidnapping plot's muscle actually comes from is covered in the Conflict & Threats chapter; this is the political and legal shape the scheme itself takes.

## Crime and the Law

Roman justice runs in two genuinely different stages, and Gens keeps them as separate systems rather than blending them into one pipeline.

### Crime, Punishment & Imprisonment — the power to act

Anyone holding real authority over another person — a household head over a dependent, a magistrate over anyone in their jurisdiction — can imprison, exile, or even execute them at essentially any time, without waiting on a formal trial. The action itself is never the gate. What matters is whether a real **Punishable Offense** sits on the target's record — a conviction, a caught scheme, a discovered affair, a captured enemy. Act against someone with a real offense behind you, and the fallout is small and contained. Act against someone with no such record, and you trigger the full weight of an **unjust** action: a direct hit to your own standing, a serious rift with the target's family, and a real chance of provoking a feud.

Sentencing runs on a historically grounded two-tier system. **Honestiores** — senators, equestrians, decurions, soldiers — face fines, milder or harsher exile (*relegatio*, *deportatio*), loss of rank, or, facing near-certain execution, a dignified suicide that spares the family's estate and standing. **Humiliores** — everyone else — face a harsher range: flogging, forced labor in the mines, being legally reduced to slavery as a sentence in its own right, condemnation to the beasts, or crucifixion.

**Detention** is a tracked status held separately from enslavement — the real Roman *carcer* mostly held people awaiting trial or ransom rather than serving a long sentence, and Gens keeps that shape rather than inventing a modern-style prison term. A detained person can escape, be interrogated (torture a serious, legally required tool for enslaved witnesses' testimony), and be **ransomed** or simply released as mercy.

Fabricating a Punishable Offense against an innocent target is its own scheme — indistinguishable from a real one until discovered, at which point it becomes the worst-case outcome available, turning the original action retroactively unjust and landing hard on whoever manufactured it. See [Crime, Punishment & Imprisonment](../design/gens-crime-punishment-imprisonment-design.md).

### Legal & Court — the trial itself

**Legal & Court** owns everything Crime & Punishment leaves untouched: the actual filing, evidence-gathering, hearing, and verdict. Routine disputes — a broken contract, a boundary quarrel, a debt gone bad — resolve in a single weighted check the same session they're filed, weighing the presiding magistrate's temperament (bribable, strictly honorable, corrupt) against each party's standing and case strength; a Dismissed verdict costs the filer a real embarrassment, so filing a hopeless case isn't free even when it's meant purely to hurt the other side. Major cases — a contested inheritance, a capital charge — run a genuine multi-stage process: filing, evidence-and-testimony with counter-play on both sides, a Hearing, and a Ruling spanning real outcomes rather than a flat win-or-lose.

Presiding is a perk of local office — your magistracy lets you judge routine cases, though you're automatically recused the moment your own household is a party. Bribery, your own Dignitas, and a favor called in from a legally-specialized client all shift a case's odds before the merits are weighed, an honest acknowledgment that Roman justice was never blind.

*Patria potestas* — a household head's authority over their own dependents — sits outside this system almost entirely: no court can formally override it, however severe the exercise. The only real check is social — a lasting relationship scar and a Dignitas cost scaled to the act — and a case can still be *brought* around such an act with essentially no chance of winning, purely as public embarrassment. See [Legal & Court](../design/gens-legal-court-design.md).

## Governing Your Household and Your World

### Policies & Edicts

Running an estate means setting standing dials rather than reacting one crisis at a time. **Standing Policies** are continuous — tax rates, how you treat dependent laborers, religious spending, recruitment posture, and more — active until changed. **Funded Actions** are one-off spends for an immediate, contained payoff: games, a festival, a public work, grain relief during a famine. **Edicts** are rare, dramatic, single-stroke proclamations with lasting effect and real risk — forgiving debts across the board, a sweeping grant of citizenship, a mass manumission, or, darkest of all, a **Proscription** stripping a named rival of legal protection outright and unsettling every other great house watching.

Your accumulated pattern of choices doesn't just sit there — it slowly resolves into a household **Doctrine**, an emergent identity (the Old Blood, the Mercantile Dynasty, the Exploiter House, and others) that deepens the longer you sustain it, eventually unlocking a unique capstone ability and, if a new head keeps faith with it across a real succession, a rarer **Apex** tier. Pulling toward one identity pulls, in a real and felt way, against some others. A **Playbook** saves a full policy configuration for one-action recall, so this much customization doesn't become a chore. See [Policies & Edicts](../design/gens-policies-edicts-design.md).

### Steward and Council auto-management

You can't personally attend to every decision while traveling, governing a second settlement, or serving as regent for a minor heir — so you delegate. A **Steward** (or, once your household has grown large enough to fill several specialist positions, a genuine **Council**) runs day-to-day business according to an **Autonomy Level** you set: **Conservative** (only the most routine upkeep, everything else waits), **Standard** (sensible handling, anything genuinely consequential still held for your return), or **Full Autonomy** (real independent judgment, short of a fixed list — declaring an edict, arranging a marriage, starting a war — no steward ever decides without you).

Delegating isn't risk-free by design. Your steward's competence determines how well routine decisions land, and their **Loyalty** is a standing risk: a resentful or disloyal steward can skim funds, embezzle at real scale, or actively sabotage your household on a rival's behalf. A known absence is exactly the opening a rival house will try to exploit. On your return, or at a regency's end, you receive a full narrative **Return Report** covering everything that happened — including any betrayal uncovered. See [Steward/Council Auto-Management](../design/gens-steward-council-auto-management-design.md).

## Staying in Touch

Not every relationship requires physically traveling to maintain. **Correspondence & Letters** is the asynchronous, lower-stakes channel running alongside travel rather than replacing it — genuine letters, in genuine period form, taking real time to arrive and carrying their own risks (interception, forgery, a letter arriving to find its recipient already moved on).

Your **Inbox** is a real two-way channel: patrons, rivals, and distant family can write to you, and how you answer — promptly and generously, coldly, or not at all — has a real, felt effect on the relationship. Beyond the obvious uses (petitioning a patron, negotiating remotely, courting from afar), correspondence covers **News & Gossip** (updates on distant family and events without a special trip), **Written Instructions** to a distant appointee — the same mechanism used to adjust a steward's autonomy level mid-absence — and simple **Condolence or Congratulation** letters marking a distant birth, death, or marriage.

One honest limit: several frontier peoples, most notably the druidic culture of the Gauls, genuinely resisted committing anything of substance to writing. Ordinary trade correspondence with them works fine, but any negotiation that actually matters may simply not translate to a letter — for those peoples, physically traveling remains the only real channel. See [Correspondence & Letters](../design/gens-correspondence-letters-design.md).
