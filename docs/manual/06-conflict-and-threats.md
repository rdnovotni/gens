# Conflict & Threats

No estate in *Gens* sits perfectly safe. Beyond your gate are rival households chasing the same land and offices you are, bandits working the roads, pirates working the coasts, and — inside your own gate, if you push your workforce too hard — the possibility that the people you own rise against you. This chapter covers how your household fights, and the four faces of organized threat you'll spend a playthrough managing: raiding, famous outlaws, mass slave revolt, and the targeted kidnapping of someone you love.

## Military & Combat

### Two ways to raise a sword

You can build military strength in two genuinely different directions, and neither one is the "correct" choice.

The **Estate Force** is your own private army — squads of infantry, cavalry, siege engineers, or militia that you name, arm, and command directly. Building one starts with a Barracks, which you can later upgrade into a Garrison and then a Fortress; each tier raises how many squads you can field and how sharp they can get. Every squad tracks its own **manpower**, **equipment** (better weapons and armor from your Armory, horses from your Stable, siege engines from your Siege Workshop all matter), **readiness** (which rises with training downtime and falls after deployment), and **morale** (which is a separate concern from readiness — a squad whose morale collapses can suffer real **desertion**, soldiers simply walking off, distinct from combat losses).

You have three ways to actually put manpower into those squads. Building the Force slowly through ordinary recruitment is cheap and loyal but takes time. A **Muster** temporarily calls your settlement's own veteran population back to active duty for a specific campaign — they arrive well-trained but the civilian labor pool shrinks for as long as they're mustered. **Mercenaries** are the fast option: they arrive at full readiness with no Barracks investment needed at all, at a steep ongoing price and with shallow loyalty — an unpaid or badly-beaten mercenary company deserts far more readily than your own people would. None of the three dominates; each trades speed against cost against reliability differently, and which one makes sense depends on how you actually got into a crisis in the first place.

Recruiting for real militia duty (estate defense, keeping order at home) draws on your lowest-status free workers or a temporary pull from enslaved labor, and carries no particular stigma — every Roman household armed itself this way. Anything offensive, or any real Roman military career, requires free status. Arming enslaved people for offense, or as a genuine last stand, is a real and rare measure the game marks as unusual rather than routine — though a slave who fights well under those circumstances is also a strong candidate for being freed on the spot.

The **household rank ladder** — Recruit, Optio, Centurion, and eventually **Praefectus** commanding your whole Force — is mostly an informal climb, capped by a genuine court appointment at the top. Separately, a sufficiently talented and well-sponsored household member can pursue an actual **Roman military career** instead: Auxiliary or Legionary service, rising toward Tribune and Legate, or rising through the ranks as an enlisted Centurion regardless of birth. This is a real and deliberate tradeoff, not a strictly better option lurking behind the modest one — keeping your best fighter as Praefectus means direct control and steady, personal value at home; sending them into Roman Service trades that control away entirely for a shot at real glory, citizenship for a non-citizen, and the kind of prestige your household's own Chronicle will remember for generations. Its rare, ultimate payoff — an actual triumph — is exactly what a Triumphal Arch on your own land is built to commemorate.

### How a fight actually resolves

Every engagement in the game — a border skirmish, a bandit raid, a private feud with a rival house, even the arena — runs through the same **Combat Resolution Engine**, so once you understand it once, you understand it everywhere.

A **Force** (a collection of squads) or a **Fleet** (its naval mirror, built from your Shipyard) faces an opposing Force. A named **commander** is never optional in any meaningful sense — going in without one resolves against a flat, unfavorable default, which is the game's way of making "who's actually leading this" a real decision rather than an afterthought. Where a commander is present, their martial skill, personality (boldness, level-headedness), and traits (Strategist, Battle-Hardened, Inspiring Commander, and their darker counterparts) all weigh directly on the outcome — and this cuts both ways: the enemy commander, whether a rival noble, a pirate captain, or a rebel leader, is a full character too, generated the moment you actually face them, not an anonymous stat block. That's what makes **Reconnaissance** worthwhile — scouting before you commit reveals some of what you're actually up against, though it costs time the enemy can use to prepare.

Terrain matters the way you'd expect (cavalry favors open ground, infantry favors hills and forest, siege gear is useless against anything unfortified), and the engine weighs manpower, equipment, readiness, the commander, and terrain together into an effective strength for each side — then rolls with real variance, so the stronger side is favored but never guaranteed to win. Outcomes come in five real flavors, not a binary win/lose: a **Decisive Victory** costs you little and opens the door to spoils and captives; a **Costly Victory** achieves the goal but bleeds both sides; a **Repulsed/Stalemate** simply leaves the underlying problem unresolved; a **Defeat** routs your force and exposes any present commander to capture; and a **Catastrophic Defeat** can effectively destroy the force outright.

Combat gets personal in a few specific ways worth knowing about. A commander of sufficient rank can keep a small personal **Retinue** — companions or family fighting at their side who can absorb the blow that would otherwise land on the commander. Either commander can issue a formal **Battlefield Duel**, a single-combat challenge whose outcome can swing an entire engagement's morale or even end it outright. A rollable table of mid-fight events — a lost standard, a rousing speech, a soldier's sacrifice, foul weather — adds texture and real consequence beyond the raw numbers. And when a fight goes against you, the stakes are genuinely personal: a present commander can be permanently injured, captured (opening a ransom negotiation, or worse), or killed outright — including your own played character, whose battlefield death hands off through your succession rules exactly as any other death would, with no special safety net. Leading from the front for the morale bonus it grants is a real risk you choose to take, not one the game quietly shields you from.

A siege against a fortified target isn't a single roll — it's a sustained state that drains both the besieger's supply and the besieged settlement's granary over months, and ends one of three ways: **Relief** (a friendly force breaks it), **Negotiated Surrender** (reduced spoils, the population spared, the least costly outcome to your standing), or **the Sack** (maximum spoils and captives, at a real and lasting cost to your reputation). Everything an engagement produces compiles into a readable **Battle Report** afterward — your actual point of contact with most routine engagements; a major campaign or a duel you personally called is worth watching unfold in real time instead.

Deployment comes in several shapes: **Defense**, **Suppression** of trouble already inside your own territory, **Offense/Campaign** against an outside target (or as your contribution to an actual Roman war effort), **Private Feuds** between rival houses fought entirely outside Rome's sanction, and **Naval** engagements between fleets. A victory yields real spoils and captives — war captives feed directly into your household's labor supply, and a captured enemy notable is a real person you now have a choice about, not a checkbox.

For the full mechanical detail behind squads, the engine's resolution steps, and siege mechanics, see [Military & Combat](../design/gens-military-combat-design.md).

## Piracy & Banditry

Not every threat to your household comes from a rival family or a foreign war. **Confederations** — organized bands of bandits working the roads from a hidden forest or hill camp, or pirates working the sea from a hidden cove — are a standing, human threat layer distinct from bad weather or blight: a menace that scales with how much you've invested in security, and one you can intercept, buy off, retaliate against, or simply be worn down by if you ignore it.

A Confederation isn't a fixed, static nuisance. Left unchallenged, it grows bolder and more dangerous over time — an ignored threat never stays a comfortably safe one to keep ignoring. Recently bribed off or roughed up without being finished, it shrinks back and grows more cautious instead. What it targets is broad: your trade caravans, your livestock, your unprotected settlers, or a traveling member of your own family.

You have real options when a raid threatens or lands. A well-defended target can simply repel or capture the raiders outright — a captured raider is a real person, available to be handed to the courts or, in a fittingly circular irony, sold into slavery. You can pay a **one-time bribe** to call off a specific brewing raid once you've learned of it, or arrange a **standing tribute** that keeps you entirely off a particular Confederation's target list for as long as you keep paying — a real protection racket, and one that costs you socially if it ever becomes public knowledge, since a respectable household quietly paying criminals for safety is not a good look. Or you can go on the offensive: **Retaliation** means finding the Confederation's actual hideout (not automatically known — you'll need to scout or spy for it first) and striking it directly, and a retaliation pushed hard enough can eliminate the Confederation from the world entirely.

The relationship can run the other way too. Your own Force or Fleet can raid as the aggressor exactly like any other offensive campaign — but doing so openly, under your own household's name, is a real scandal proportional to how public it becomes. Routing the raid through a Confederation you've already dealt with instead buys you deniability at the cost of a cut of the take, and at a real risk that your hired intermediary simply keeps the proceeds and tells you it failed. Go further still and you can build a genuine standing relationship with a Confederation: buying goods and captives they've raided, or even a full alliance where they supply auxiliary raiders for your own campaigns or intelligence on a rival's vulnerable shipments. None of this is free of exposure — being caught consorting with known criminals is its own scandal.

Your best defense is simply investment: a Vigil or Praefectus Vigilum watching your streets, a Navarchus commanding coastal patrols, watchtowers and city walls, a standing fleet if you're exposed to the sea. Real security investment does double duty — it makes you a worse target to begin with, and it makes you far likelier to win outright if targeted anyway.

For the full anatomy of a Confederation, bribery and tribute mechanics, and the raiding-both-ways rules, see [Piracy & Banditry](../design/gens-piracy-banditry-design.md).

## Bandit Lords & Outlaw Factions

Most Confederations stay exactly that: an ordinary, faceless raiding nuisance you manage as a recurring line item. Occasionally, one doesn't. A **Bandit Lord of Note** is a Confederation whose leader has become genuinely, personally famous — someone the whole province has an opinion about, drawn from one of three real historical patterns Rome actually produced.

The **Social Bandit** preys selectively on the wealthy while sparing common folk and craftsmen, and in doing so builds real goodwill among your settlement's lower classes — goodwill that makes suppressing him genuinely complicated, since informants go quiet and a heavy-handed crackdown against a popular outlaw can cost you standing of its own. The **Guerrilla Resistance Leader** wages a drawn-out hit-and-run campaign rather than ever meeting you in open battle. The **Pirate Confederation Chief** commands a network brazen and dangerous enough that only a genuinely large, well-resourced military campaign — not cleverness — ever actually ends it. Which archetype a given Bandit Lord draws on depends partly on where he operates and partly on his own personal character.

A Bandit Lord who survives repeated engagements against your household becomes a genuine **Nemesis** — his fame grows with every failed attempt to end him, and your household's standing with him turns openly, mutually hostile. Each archetype has its own historically fitting downfall: the Social Bandit falls to infiltration (made harder by the goodwill he's built), the Guerrilla Leader falls when you successfully bribe one of his own lieutenants to betray him (a real if morally murky victory, since it costs you a little of your own honor), and the Pirate Chief falls only to the sheer weight of a real, well-resourced campaign. Any archetype can, in principle, be approached by a method other than its own — just at worse odds.

Should a household member ever be captured and ransomed by a Bandit Lord specifically, the story doesn't necessarily end when the ransom's paid: a sufficiently bold and vengeful survivor can pursue a **Settle the Score** ambition against the very outlaw who once held them — a guaranteed highlight for your Chronicle regardless of how things go afterward. Capture isn't always the end either: a defeated Bandit Lord can be tried and sentenced like any criminal, or, for a very public reckoning, sent to die in the arena. A sufficiently legendary Lord's name can outlive him too, surfacing years later in local memory, or as a **Copycat Successor** invoking his old reputation without literally being the same organization.

For the full archetype table, the Nemesis mechanic, and each downfall path in detail, see [Bandit Lords & Outlaw Factions](../design/gens-bandit-lords-outlaw-factions-design.md).

## Servile Wars & Slave Revolts

Individual unrest among the people you enslave — punishment, flight, day-to-day resentment — is a household-scale concern covered in the [Estate & Economy chapter](04-estate-and-economy.md). This section covers what happens when that unrest stops being many separate private calculations and becomes one organized, collective act.

Real Roman history gives this its shape directly: the two Sicilian Servile Wars of the second century BC, and — far more consequentially for anyone playing in Italy — the Third Servile War of 73–71 BC, Spartacus's own revolt, which began with an escape from a gladiator school and grew into a force that beat multiple Roman armies before its brutal suppression. Your game tracks a **Regional Unrest** reading distinct from any single household's own unrest score — it factors in the average unrest across every enslaved population in a settlement, how concentrated that enslaved population is relative to the free citizens watching over it, and how strong a garrison or military presence is actually standing guard. A dangerously high Regional Unrest reading is dry tinder; it takes an actual **Spark** to set it off — a severe punishment carried out publicly, a specific charismatic individual reaching a breaking point, a garrison pulled away at the wrong moment, a famine, or a rival house deliberately fanning the flames against you.

A spark landing on low Regional Unrest just fizzles into an ordinary, contained incident. Landing on high Regional Unrest, it can escalate through three real scales: a **Local Uprising** (one estate, handled much like an ordinary punishment-and-flight incident, just bigger), a **Regional Revolt** (multiple estates, genuinely requiring a real military response), and — rarest and most consequential — a full **Servile War**, province-spanning and capable of requiring Rome's own direct intervention, the kind of event your Chronicle remembers a household by for generations. Any revolt at Regional scale or above produces a real, named **Leader** who builds fame the way a gladiator does, and who grows more dangerous the longer they stay free.

When a revolt is underway, you have real choices, not one obviously correct button: put it down militarily, negotiate an end to it (rare and socially costly, but a legitimate option for a household that genuinely can't win by force), or abandon the estate outright to save your people's lives at the cost of the property. Suppressing a revolt successfully brings relief and a little restored standing, but never for free — you permanently lose the labor of everyone killed putting it down, and a defeated survivor may face the same grim, historically accurate fate as Spartacus's own men, stated plainly and without dwelling on it. Going badly, at Servile War scale, is a real possibility too: an overrun estate, family members captured or killed, even a forced abandonment of the whole settlement. And a region that's suffered a revolt carries an elevated unrest baseline afterward that doesn't fully recede — a lasting argument for actually changing course rather than rebuilding and repeating the choices that caused it.

Your best tools against all of this are ones you already have: sensible treatment of your workforce, a visible garrison, freeing your most dangerously unhappy or ambitious people before they become a spark, and — if you're pious — reading your household's omens for early warning. You can also turn this weapon around: a skilled operative can deliberately incite unrest in a rival's own household, a genuine and quietly devastating act of sabotage if it goes undiscovered.

For Regional Unrest's full mechanics, the Spark table, and the three-tier escalation in detail, see [Servile Wars & Slave Revolts](../design/gens-servile-wars-slave-revolts-design.md).

## When it's personal: Kidnap & Ransom

Every threat in this chapter can, in its sharpest form, come down to one specific person you care about rather than your estate in the abstract. A pirate Confederation can carry off a traveling family member. A rival house can pay a bandit gang for a **Targeted Contract** against your heir specifically — to kidnap, kill, or even deliver them into slavery as a deliberate humiliation. And a Bandit Lord who's ever held one of your people captive can become the target of a very personal reckoning once they're free again.

The full mechanics of a kidnapping — how ransom negotiations actually work, what can be demanded beyond simple coin, how to keep or rescue a hostage, and what happens to a captive during their confinement — get their own dedicated treatment.
