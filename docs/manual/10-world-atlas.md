# The World Atlas

*Gens* takes place across the real, sprawling geography of the Roman world, in the long stretch of history running from the reforms of the Gracchi in 133 BC through the settled prosperity of the Pax Romana and onward, in the design corpus behind the game, all the way to Justinian's reconquests around AD 565. The default starting era is the Pax Romana itself — chosen because a household needs stable ground to grow into a settlement or a dynasty — but nothing about the setting is welded to one decade. A household can just as easily be founded in the raw, unfinished frontier colonization of the late Republic, or in the harder, more anxious world of the early Dominate, without any of the game's core systems breaking underneath it.

Wherever and whenever your household begins, the world around it is not a painted backdrop. Rival houses in your region are pursuing the same land, offices, and marriages you are, whether or not you ever cross paths with them. Real cities, ports, temples, and fortresses anchor your travels, your correspondence, and your ambitions — the same way a real map of the ancient Mediterranean would, without asking you to march an army across a hex grid to get there. This chapter is your reference for that wider world: the eighteen regions you can found a household in, the peoples, faiths, animals, and plants that fill it out, and the long historical timeline the whole design draws on.

## Choosing a Starting Region

The single biggest choice you make before your first session even begins is where your household actually sits in the Roman world. Region determines your starting terrain, your local economy, your access to Roman politics, your neighbors — hostile, friendly, or merely curious — and the local population your household will spend a lifetime living alongside. It is a genuinely separate choice from your household's own culture: a Roman family can found an estate on the Gallic frontier just as easily as a newly-enfranchised Gallic family can build a Roman-style villa on native ground, and the game is built to support both without friction. Wherever you settle, the people around you are never a monolith — every region's population carries real outliers, from a Syrian merchant working the Gallic frontier to a Hellenic tutor teaching in Latium, because the actual ancient Mediterranean was that connected and that mixed.

Some regions ask more of you than others. Wherever Roman authority is recent, contested, or administratively thin, your household tracks **two separate reputations at once** rather than one: your standing with Rome, and your standing with the local, non-Roman population around you — a mechanic the game calls **Reputation Duality**. The two axes can move in completely opposite directions. A household that squeezes a province for every coin and recruit Rome wants will often find its standing at home climbing while its neighbors' trust collapses; a household that governs gently and tolerates local custom builds real local loyalty at the cost of looking unambitious, or worse, insufficiently useful, to the people back in Rome. Neither path is the "correct" one — both are real, playable identities.

Reputation Duality doesn't look the same everywhere it appears, because the real history behind it isn't uniform either:

- **Full** — Rome's authority and local loyalty are two fully independent, actively diverging concerns for the whole span of a typical playthrough. This is the shape the mechanic was built around, and it belongs to the frontier regions that never really finish being conquered.
- **Tapering** — a region with a real, dateable end to its conquest. Standing starts genuinely divided in the early years and gradually converges toward a single, settled reputation the longer a household's start date sits after that closing date.
- **Permanent Structural** — a region conquered suddenly and completely, with no drawn-out war to taper away from, but where Rome deliberately kept native institutions and administration separate from ordinary provincial life for the entire span of the game. The tension here never fades because it was never temporary to begin with.
- **Localized** — a single selectable region that actually contains multiple very different local textures at once, so how "live" the tension feels depends entirely on where your household's own life and holdings actually sit, not on one dial for the whole region.
- **None** — an honest finding, not a gap: several regions are simply calm, thoroughly integrated, or old enough that any historical tension has long since faded.
- **Great Power Allegiance** — one region's household doesn't reconcile local standing with Rome at all, but instead tracks its position between two rival outside empires.

A handful of regions sit outside the Reputation Duality framework entirely, because they were never Roman territory in the first place — there, a household's default identity isn't even a Roman colonist, but a native noble house or a resident foreign merchant living under someone else's law.

Every region also carries its own **Gazetteer** — a curated set of real historical places: provincial capitals, ports, legionary fortresses, sanctuaries, and market towns your household can travel to, transact with, or watch a rival house claim as its own seat. Your own estate stays as abstract as ever — you never place it at an exact coordinate — but the world around it is concrete and real, the same real place from playthrough to playthrough even as what's actually happening there changes with every game.

The regions below are grouped roughly by how central they are to the game's initial roster: the four original launch regions (alongside Italy, which is itself two selectable regions), five further regions that quickly followed, and seven more individual regions rounding out a genuinely large starting roster.

## Launch Regions

### Italian Heartland — Latium

Latium is Rome's own backyard: the Tiber's fertile floodplain, a modest but real coastline near Ostia, and rolling hill country studded with towns older than Rome's own dominance over them. It is the single most expensive, most intensively farmed land in the game, and the fastest, surest route into the *cursus honorum* — a Latium household clears Rome's notice faster than any other region's can. What makes a Latium start distinctive is how little untouched land is left to expand into, and how thoroughly its wealth depends on things it can't fully control: the capital's own hunger for imported grain leaves even the wealthiest Latium estate exposed to a bad season on someone else's farm. There's no local population to hold a second loyalty with here, no frontier, no legions — the risk is slow and structural rather than sudden, and the opportunity is proximity to power itself.

### Italian Heartland — Campania

Campania is Latium's economic mirror: the Bay of Naples's volcanic soil makes it a genuine grain and wine exporter rather than an importer, its ports made it Italy's real commercial heart for generations, and its population is genuinely layered — old Oscan and Samnite roots, a real, continuously Greek city at Naples, foreign cults taking root at Puteoli well before they'd dare set up in Latium. Political life here runs through leisure-villa society at Baiae as much as through any formal office. Campania trades Latium's political centrality for wealth and cosmopolitan texture, at the cost of the single most dramatic standing risk on the entire roster: Vesuvius broods over the region's richest towns, a real, mechanically live threat baked into the household's very starting location rather than a distant abstraction.

### Gallic Frontier

Gaul stays a single selectable region on purpose, spanning the long-Romanized south around Massilia and Narbo Martius and the genuinely raw, Rhine-adjacent Three Gauls further north. This is the frontier Reputation Duality was written for: cheap and abundant land, thin markets outside a handful of real cities, and a standing security cost no Italian household ever has to budget for. A Gallic household answers to two audiences that can easily fall out of step — Rome, and the region's own still-largely-Gallic population, whose native Druidic tradition Roman authorities actively suppress rather than merely tolerate. Beyond the Rhine sits a real, permanently tense external neighbor in the Germanic tribes. In exchange for all of that risk, a Gallic start offers this roster's clearest path for a *novus homo* household to rise on its own merits, far from Latium's crowded political field.

### Iberian Colony

Iberia pairs a calm, wealthy, senatorial south (Baetica) with a more recently pacified, garrisoned north (Tarraconensis) under one selectable region. Its mountains hold some of the richest gold and silver deposits in the ancient Mediterranean, worked at real, brutal human cost that the game doesn't look away from, and its river valley supports a genuine olive-oil export economy famous enough to have physically built a hill in Rome out of its own broken shipping jars. Iberia has no external frontier to speak of — once its real conquest closes, there's no one left beyond the border to negotiate with — so its Reputation Duality tapers rather than staying permanently live, converging toward calm the later a playthrough's start date sits after that closing war. A colonial-born household here has a real, documented path all the way to Rome's highest office, upending any assumption that "colony" means a lower ceiling.

### Greek East

Greek East is where outside powers, including Rome's own civil wars, come to settle their fights on someone else's soil — a real, historically distinctive identity no other region shares. Its wealth runs through trade, craft, and cultural prestige rather than raw acreage, and it hosts the densest concentration of famous religious and educational sites in the game: Delphi's oracle, Athens's Academy, Olympia's ancient games. It carries no Reputation Duality at all — the population here is thoroughly, calmly integrated — but it is the only launch region bordering a genuine Great Power rather than a tribal frontier: Parthia itself, reached through the militarized Cappadocian edge of an otherwise demilitarized, sophisticated heartland. The stakes of getting that relationship wrong are correspondingly larger than anywhere else on the early roster.

## Extended Regions

### Egypt

Egypt runs on rules no other region shares. It was never governed as an ordinary province at all — senators needed the Emperor's own personal permission just to enter, and the territory functioned as something closer to the Emperor's private estate than Roman territory in the usual sense. Its agriculture inverts the game's usual disaster logic: drought here comes from a poor Nile flood rather than a lack of rain, and the river's own famously gentle inundation makes ordinary flood risk lower than the terrain would otherwise suggest. Egypt's local-standing tension is Permanent Structural — sudden and total conquest, followed by a native priesthood and administration Rome deliberately kept separate for the rest of the timeline. To the south lies Kush, a real, sophisticated, sovereign neighbor Rome fought once and then treated as something close to an equal ever after — the richest single grain-producing region in the game, anchored by Alexandria's unmatched intellectual prestige.

### Syria / The Levant

No other region asks one selectable choice to hold this much internal variety. Syria proper is Rome's calm, thoroughly integrated administrative and military heartland; Judaea is a real client-turned-province carrying a singular monotheistic tradition and two separate historical revolts within the game's own range; and the desert caravan cities of Petra and Palmyra are wealthy, largely self-governing trading partners rather than conquered subjects. Reputation Duality here is genuinely Localized — none in Syria proper, the sharpest tension on the entire roster in Judaea, and a cooperative, non-adversarial partnership in the desert cities — so how tense your household's life feels depends entirely on where its attention actually sits. This is also the region facing Parthia's heaviest frontier sector, anchored at the Euphrates crossing of Zeugma.

### Britannia

Britannia is a harder, later-arriving version of the Gallic frontier's own promise: conquest starts three generations later, land is the cheapest on the entire roster, and the frontier never actually closes — Hadrian's Wall is a monument to giving up on finishing the job, not a victory arch. Reputation Duality here runs Full, and settles more slowly than Gaul's own, because Boudicca's revolt proves that even the supposedly pacified lowland south was never as loyal as it looked, seventeen years after the original conquest. Britain borders two very different unconquered neighbors — a permanently hostile Caledonia to the north and a distant, essentially peaceful Ireland — and carries an unusually heavy three-legion garrison that exists as much to watch its own province as to watch the frontier.

### Anatolia / Asia Minor

By the time this region came to be designed, its most famous cities — Rhodes, Pergamon, and the Cappadocian frontier — already belonged to Greek East. What's left is still substantial: Galatia, where migrated Celtic tribes became something genuinely new rather than simply assimilating; Cilicia, the south coast that was the Mediterranean's most notorious pirate haven before Pompey's suppression; Bithynia-Pontus, whose real surviving correspondence between a provincial governor and his emperor gives this region an unmatched window into how governance-by-letter actually worked; and a wealthy western coast anchored at Ephesus, home to one of the ancient world's Seven Wonders. Reputation Duality here is honestly None — no live frontier, no lingering resentment, a genuinely calm region rather than one straining to manufacture tension it doesn't have.

### The Balkans

The Balkans span the Adriatic coast, the Danube frontier, and Thrace, but its defining feature is Dacia: real Roman territory only from Trajan's conquest in AD 106, meaning a household starting earlier in the timeline simply cannot settle there at all — it's a live, external neighbor rather than a home. Reputation Duality reuses Syria's Localized shape: calm in the long-settled south, tapering in Pannonia around a real, dateable revolt, and fully live in Dacia for essentially the entire span any Dacia-based playthrough could ever cover, since the province is annexed too late in the timeline to ever fully settle before the game's usual range ends. Dacia's own real gold wealth, once available, is a genuine late-game prize unlike anything the earlier regions offer.

## Further Regions

### Alpine Provinces

Raetia and Noricum are the calmest, smallest, and most vertical territory on the roster — the only region reachable overland from Italy, Gaul, and the Balkans alike without crossing open water. The two provinces joined Rome within a year of each other by two very different paths, one by conquest and one by a peaceful absorption of an old allied kingdom, but both settled into lasting calm almost immediately. Its wealth is industrial rather than agricultural or trade-hub in the usual sense — famously excellent Noric steel and an ancient salt-mining tradition older than Rome itself — plus a genuine role as the connective corridor between Italy and the Rhine-Danube frontier system. Reputation Duality is honestly None.

### Armenia

Armenia was never quite a province. For almost the entire span of the game, it existed as a real Contested Buffer kingdom that Rome and Parthia spent centuries installing and deposing rival kings over, neither side ever definitively winning. A household here has two genuinely different lives available: a Roman diplomatic family working a Legate's posting, the safer and more straightforward option, or a native Armenian noble house navigating which patron actually serves its family's interests at any given moment — potentially switching allegiance more than once across a long playthrough. In place of Reputation Duality, Armenia tracks something new: **Great Power Allegiance**, standing with Rome and Parthia simultaneously, with the region's own ruling king's loyalty as the live, changeable stage that standing plays out on.

### Mesopotamia

Mesopotamia is this roster's most extreme structural departure: real Roman administration here, by historical default, lasted exactly two years, briefly created and then deliberately abandoned by Hadrian. A household that chooses this region should expect its entire premise to carry a foreknown expiration date, with most of the actual gameplay built around what it does with that knowledge — including one carefully bounded, explicitly-labeled exception where a household's own extraordinary governance can plausibly persuade history's outcome to bend, even though the real emperors' own lives and deaths never do. There simply isn't time for Reputation Duality to develop under the honest default; it only begins accumulating, and then at full intensity, if a household manages to extend the occupation.

### Nubia

Nubia inverts this roster's basic premise entirely: your household is the outsider in someone else's empire, not the reverse. The real Kingdom of Kush was never conquered by Rome — the two powers fought one real war to a rough draw, settled it by negotiated peace, and spent the rest of the timeline as respectful trading partners. A household here defaults to a genuine Kushite noble family, embedded in a sophisticated, independent African civilization with its own writing system, monumental architecture, and a real tradition of ruling queens holding sovereign power in their own right — or, as a real but more unusual alternative, a resident foreign merchant living under Kushite law. Reputation Duality simply doesn't apply, for the simplest possible reason: no Roman administration ever existed here to be dual with.

### Arabia Felix

South Arabia — the real incense kingdoms of Saba, Himyar, Qataban, and Hadramawt — was never Roman territory, and unlike Nubia's negotiated peace, Rome's one real military attempt here was an outright failure that was never repeated. The honest default builds on genuine, extensive trade contact: a resident merchant household working the frankincense-and-myrrh network, or a native South Arabian noble house entirely independent of Rome. For players who want an actual Roman colonial premise here, the region also offers a clearly labeled counterfactual layer imagining that one failed Roman expedition had instead succeeded — kept entirely separate from, and never assumed by, the honest historical default.

### Bosporan Kingdom

The Bosporan Kingdom, around the Black Sea's Kerch Strait, is the one client relationship on the entire roster that never converts to full annexation across the whole span of the game — a genuinely permanent, stable, and low-friction arrangement. Its client kings actually took Roman imperial names as part of their own royal titles, a real, visible blend of Greek civic heritage, Scythian steppe connection, and Roman patronage all held at once, rather than a choice a household has to make between them. Its own frontier, facing Sarmatian and Scythian steppe peoples, is the kingdom's own to manage rather than Rome's, complete with real intermarriage between Bosporan elites and steppe nobility softening what could otherwise be pure hostility. Reputation Duality is honestly None; there's nothing here that needs a new mechanic.

### Sicily

Sicily became Rome's very first province in 241 BC, more than a century before the game's own timeline even begins — the oldest, calmest, most thoroughly settled "None" on the entire roster. It's genuinely instructive rather than dull: Rome kept the previous Greek king's own tax code running rather than replacing it, one of the clearest illustrations anywhere in the game that Roman rule didn't always mean imposing a uniform system, and Sicily is where Rome's whole later pattern of depending on someone else's grain actually began, well before Egypt or North Africa took up the same role. Mount Etna gives the island its own volcanic hazard, distinctly more frequent and less catastrophic than Vesuvius's singular disaster further north.

### North African Colony

North Africa carries one of the sharpest ironies in the whole game: Rome destroyed Carthage in 146 BC as completely as any conquest in the timeline, then rebuilt it within a few generations into the thriving administrative capital of a rich grain-and-olive province. The region splits internally between an old, thoroughly settled eastern core built quite literally on Carthage's ashes, and a more recently annexed western Mauretania — giving this region a doubled, two-hinged version of Iberia's tapering Reputation Duality, with the east essentially settled for the entire game and the west carrying real, live local tension much later into the timeline. A single legion garrisons the whole territory, the lightest military footprint on the roster, and its Numidian marble and yellow-toned quarries give it a distinct building-stone identity of its own.

## Peoples, Faiths, Beasts, and Plants of the Known World

The eighteen starting regions are only the ground floor. Underneath them sits a set of shared reference registries the whole game draws on — the same real cultures, faiths, animals, and plants recur across regions, buildings, trade goods, character traits, and events rather than each system inventing its own small, disconnected list.

**Cultures of the Known World** catalogs the real, playable peoples your household, your neighbors, and your rivals might belong to — 35 named cultures in all, sorted into four honest categories: Provincial (already governed by Rome, including a few, like the Etruscans, already fully absorbed before the game's own timeline even opens), Frontier (genuinely outside Roman control for some or all of the range, sometimes shifting to Provincial partway through at a real, dateable conquest), Great Power (Parthia, with Armenia sitting in its own distinct Contested Buffer position between Rome and Parthia), and Trade Contact Only (real peoples — Indian, Chinese, Sogdian, and others — Rome traded and had cultural contact with but never conquered or seriously fought). Every culture carries its own real historical grounding, its relationship to Rome, its religious tendency, and a flavor lean that colors how its people tend to behave in play — Gallic warrior-aristocracy, Punic mercantile stewardship outliving the state that once carried it, Batavian tribes simultaneously unconquered and prized as the Emperor's own personal guard. A recurring, deliberate design choice threads through the whole registry: no culture's population in any region is ever locked to a single identity. Every distribution leaves real room for outliers, because the actual ancient Mediterranean was that thoroughly interconnected.

**Religions of the Known World** does the same work for faith, cataloging 27 named traditions across four categories: State & Civic Polytheisms (Roman, Hellenic, Egyptian, Parthian), Ethnic & Tribal Polytheisms (eighteen entries, from Gallic and British Druidic tradition through Dacian Zalmoxis-worship to the Bosporan Kingdom's own three-way religious blend), Mystery Cults & Personal Devotion, and a Monotheistic Exception covering Judaism, Samaritanism, and early Christianity. The registry makes a deliberate, historically honest departure from how some other games handle faith: almost no ancient religion in this period had anything like a single central authority figure, and the game says so plainly rather than inventing one for tidiness. A shared set of real tenets — Syncretic, Exclusive, Oracular, Fire-Venerating, Persecution-Prone, and others — gives every faith a flavor layer that reads directly against existing household and event mechanics, and a small set of genuinely thin historical records (Numidian, Sarmatian, and Illyrian/Pannonian tradition among them) are honestly flagged as thin rather than dressed up with invented detail.

**The Bestiary** is the authoritative registry of the animal life your household might raise, hunt, trade, fear, or keep as a prized curiosity — domesticated livestock and working animals, wild game in both common and genuinely dangerous tiers, exotic imported beasts destined for the arena, marine and aquatic life, birds, venomous small creatures, ordinary vermin, and a confirmed roster of seventeen legendary creatures drawn from real ancient belief, from the Nemean Lion to the Phoenix to Scylla and Charybdis. A further, deliberately lighter tier sits between "confirmed real animal" and "confirmed legendary creature" for real ancient travelers' rumors of strange beasts the registry keeps honestly unresolved — led by the real, documented account of Hanno the Navigator's own "Gorillai."

**The Flora & Herbal Registry** is the Bestiary's direct sibling for anything rooted in the ground: grain and staple crops, orchard and vine cultivation, fiber and dye plants, culinary and medicinal herbs, poisonous and sacred plants, funerary flora, ornamentals, timber trees, and aromatic and incense plants — every one grounded in a real historical identity and real historical use, right down to giving Rome's own generic "Herbs" trade good and its Herbalist character trait actual named species to point to. A plant with more than one real historical use, like bay laurel's sacred and culinary roles or mistletoe's mild toxicity and Druidic sanctity, appears everywhere its real use actually earns it a place, rather than being forced into a single category.

## A Long Historical Window

Behind the map sits a genuinely long historical timeline the game's design corpus tracks in real, dated detail — a two-part chronicle running from the Gracchi's reforms in 133 BC through the Severan era around AD 235, and then, in an extended companion volume, onward through the Crisis of the Third Century, the Dominate, the Constantinian shift, the fall of the Western Empire, and Justinian's own sixth-century reconquests, closing out around AD 565. Between the two documents, the timeline names over sixty real historical figures — emperors in unbroken succession from Augustus onward, but also rebels, generals, physicians, and jurists whose lives can color the game even when they never anchor a specific dated event — and more than a hundred real, dated historical events in total. Most of that timeline sits outside any single playthrough's own reach, but it gives the game's default Pax Romana setting real depth on both sides: a household can push its own start date earlier, into the raw colonization of the late Republic, or later, toward the anxieties of the early Dominate, and find the world around it still behaving as a real, coherent history rather than a frozen backdrop built for one decade alone.
