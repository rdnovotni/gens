# The Living World

Rome doesn't pause while you're not looking at it. Other families rise and fall, the seasons turn whether or not you've planned around them, disasters and disease strike households you've never heard of, and the wider empire lurches through wars, successions, and reforms that have nothing to do with you — until, if you get prominent enough, they suddenly do. This chapter covers everything that makes your estate feel like one household inside a much bigger world, rather than the only thing that exists in it.

## Events: how the world actually reaches you

Every month, the game rolls through a **Weighted Event Pool** — a shared bucket every system in the game feeds into, from a child's coming-of-age to a rival's raid to an omen at your household shrine. Nothing about this is arbitrary: each event carries its own eligibility conditions and weighting, drawn from the same hidden numbers that already drive everything else about your household — your relationships, your reputation, your household's religious standing, your legal footing. A household deep in debt sees different events than a flush one; a household courting scandal sees different events than a quiet one.

Events come in two flavors. **Random events** are pulled from that weighted pool with no guarantees — they might happen, they might not, and how likely they are shifts with your circumstances. **Scripted events** are different: they fire the instant a specific condition is met, no roll involved — your household's doctrine reaching its defining shape, an heir coming of age, a dated moment in real history arriving on schedule. Scripted events always land on your next Monthly Report, so nothing important gets buried by bad luck.

Events also sort by scope. A **Personal** event targets one specific person in your household — a formative moment in a child's upbringing, a flirtation, a bout of illness. A **Household** event hits the whole estate — a fire, a legal case, a succession dispute. A **Settlement** event touches the wider population around you — a wave of migrants, a bandit raid nearby. And an **Imperial** event is the wider Roman world itself intruding, covered in the next section.

Not every event resolves as a single beat and then vanishes. **Event chains** — a Scheme working toward its payoff, a disaster's aftermath compounding into new trouble, a multi-year war running its real historical course — deliver their next chapter across successive Monthly Reports, so a genuinely dramatic thread can take months or years to actually play out.

### Prominence: your own volume knob on the wider world

The more your household actually matters — your standing, your wealth, your office-holding, a doctrine your family has fully committed to, a military commission, anything that makes Rome notice you exist — the more the wider empire notices you back. This is tracked as **Prominence**, a separate measure from your general standing: standing is how well-regarded you are, Prominence is how loudly the wider political world has noticed you at all. A low-Prominence household mostly just reads about the empire in dispatches. A high-Prominence one can occasionally get pulled directly into imperial-scale drama — a personal summons to Rome, a sponsorship offer, a genuine civil-war-adjacent choice of allegiance. The wider empire's effects always flow downward to every household regardless of size; it's only the reverse — your own actions rippling upward into imperial affairs — that requires real Prominence to unlock.

### The wider empire and real history

Your playthrough runs against a real, dated Roman calendar, bounded to a specific historical stretch spanning from the late Republic's own land-reform crises through the Severan dynasty's final stable stretch before Rome's third-century troubles. Within that range you'll pass through four distinct eras, each with its own political texture — the Senate-dominated late Republic, the settling-in of the early Principate, the stable high-water-mark of the "Five Good Emperors," and the noticeably shakier Severan years. Depending on how you start your game, you can choose your starting year outright, let a scenario suggest a historically fitting one, or roll it at random.

A curated timeline of real dated events — an emperor's accession, a real war or revolt where your region and year line up with one, a natural disaster on its actual historical date, a rare religious observance — runs underneath the ordinary event pool as scripted content, firing exactly when history says it should. This is deliberate: the game wants you to feel the weight of knowing what's coming, the same way a savvy player might start their household one year before Vesuvius specifically to watch it happen, or found a family right as an emperor's reign is ending specifically to see whether it holds together through what follows. Real historical figures — emperors, generals, the era's other famous names — shape this timeline and get referenced by name, but they're never characters you can personally interact with; a private household, however prominent, realistically never gets close enough to duel or scheme against a sitting emperor. Any governor or official you deal with directly is one of the game's own generated characters filling that real office, not the documented historical person who actually held it.

Two different households starting in the same year will, by default, see the identical timeline unfold — that predictability is what makes the rare exception mean something. A household prominent and consequential enough that its own actions cross a real threshold can occasionally cause history itself to genuinely branch — a different outcome to a succession, a war that resolves differently. This is called **Divergence**, and it's meant to be vanishingly rare: most playthroughs will never see one at all, a truly outstanding household might earn exactly one across an entire campaign, and it always becomes the single most important entry your family's Chronicle will ever record.

## Time itself: three different layers

It's worth untangling something the game keeps carefully separate: there isn't one "calendar" system, there are three, each doing a different job.

**The Roman Calendar** is the structural layer — the actual months (Ianuarius through December, with two later renamed for Caesar and Augustus), and the old Roman habit of counting days backward from the Kalends, Nones, and Ides rather than counting up from the 1st. You won't be asked to date things this way in ordinary play, but formal or flavorful writing — a letter, a Chronicle entry — can use it for real period texture; the most famous date on the whole timeline, Caesar's assassination, is always "the Ides of March," never "March 15th." This layer also governs the **nundinal cycle** — Rome's real eight-day market week, whose eighth day is an actual market day with a genuine, felt uptick in local trade. If your household starts before 46 BC, you're also playing under the old, irregular pre-Julian calendar, where a religious office called the Pontifex Maximus could historically manipulate the calendar itself for political ends — a real, era-specific lever unique to an early start.

**The Seasons** are the mechanical layer sitting on top of that calendar — what time of year actually does to your household. Spring opens the campaigning and sailing seasons and is when planting happens; summer brings the grain harvest and is the driest, most fire- and drought-prone stretch; autumn brings the grape and olive harvests, rising disease risk, and the start of the sea's genuinely dangerous season; winter is when the Mediterranean sea lanes effectively close (the ancient *mare clausum*), armies stand down into winter quarters instead of campaigning, and a household without adequate stored grain feels a real, predictable "hungry gap" as the previous year's surplus runs thin. None of this is a hard lock — a desperate voyage or a stubborn winter campaign is still possible — but defying the season always carries a real, felt cost. Prices move with the seasons too: a harvest month brings a local glut and lower prices, the run-up to spring brings scarcity and higher ones.

**Events' own calendar** — the year, era, and historical timeline described above — is a third, separate thing again: it's the backbone your dated real-world content and imperial affairs run on, distinct from both the month-to-month calendar mechanics and the seasonal effects layered over them. Three different systems, three different jobs, all quietly working together under the hood.

## Rival Houses and the living world

This is the single most important idea behind everything else in this chapter, so it's worth explaining once, clearly: **the game doesn't simulate everyone at full detail all the time.** Most of the world — other noble families, ordinary households, itinerant specialists passing through, the businesses in your town — exists only as lightweight background data: a name, a rough fortune, a general trend. The instant you actually interact with one of them, or hear something specific about them, the game "promotes" it into a fully realized character or household with a real history, a real family tree, and real ongoing behavior. This is exactly the same promotion rule the game already uses for ordinary people around your estate — the game never bothers building out a full character sheet for someone you'll never meet.

Applied to rival noble families specifically, this means the overwhelming majority of gentes in the world stay **Background Houses**: a name, a rough standing trend (Rising, Established, or Declining), an approximate fortune, and one dominant flavor tag describing what the family is about. Their fortunes tick along through periodic abstract rolls — births, deaths, marriages, fortunes shifting — without you ever seeing the details. A **Rising** house is the one most likely to actually pick a fight with you or a rival, entering a marriage market or an election on its own initiative; an **Established** house is stable background scenery and the safest one to actually ally with; a **Declining** house is the natural target for absorption or a easy mark if you're looking for a fight.

The moment real contact happens — a marriage candidate drawn from that family, an election opponent, a feud, a contested plot of land — that house is promoted to a **House of Note**: a real head of household generated as a full character, a tracked identity, real evolving wealth, and genuine appearances in your Monthly Report and Dynasty Chronicle. If a House of Note goes quiet for a long stretch with nothing currently connecting it to you, it simply drops back to the lighter background tracking — its last-known state frozen in place rather than continuing to update in the background. This is also why a rival family's **Dossier** — the summary record you can pull up on any tracked house — is never guaranteed to be perfectly current: for a house you're actively dealing with it reflects the real, live state, but for one that's fallen quiet, it's only as fresh as your last actual contact or the last bit of correspondence that reached you. A rival you haven't crossed paths with in years might have genuinely changed hands, fortune, or standing since you last checked.

Rival houses don't only compete with you. They marry each other, feud with each other, and ally against each other whether or not you're watching, and a severe enough feud can leave an **Ancestral Grudge** — a standing hostility between the two houses that outlives the specific people who fought it, sometimes for generations. The world also doesn't just thin out over time: new houses rise to replace ones that go extinct, either through an ambitious family climbing high enough to found a house of its own, or through an existing house splitting when it's grown too large for one inheritance to satisfy every ambitious son.

This same sampling-and-promotion pattern — mostly background, promoted on contact — is how the game handles several other things you'll encounter elsewhere in the manual, so once you understand it here, you understand all of them:

- **Notable Households** are ordinary, non-noble families around your settlement, tracked and promoted the exact same way.
- **Wandering Populations** (below) are itinerant specialists moving through the world on their own schedule.
- **Notable Businesses** are named commercial enterprises that rise, compete, and fail using the identical logic.

Trade guilds, foreign peoples and petty kingdoms, pirate confederations, and religious institutions all get folded into this same shared framework too — each is simply a different flavor of the same underlying "Living World Actor," simulated at whatever depth your actual contact with it warrants.

## Wandering Populations

Beyond the noble houses and businesses of your own region, a smaller cast of people move through the wider world on their own professional circuit: traveling philosophers and rhetoricians touring the great schools of the Greek East, architects and engineers chasing the next big commission, merchants working a trade route, entertainer troupes booked town to town, itinerant physicians, and holy men and mystics drawn to wherever a foreign cult or a promising omen calls them. Each of these **Wanderers** carries a real, moving itinerary and a reputation that rises and falls on its own — the same Fame score described in the Games & Spectacle chapter, simply earned through travel and renown instead of the arena.

You'll usually first hear about a Wanderer through rumor — a letter or report mentioning that a celebrated rhetorician is touring the province — well before you ever cross paths with them directly, giving you a real chance to plan a trip around reaching them. Once you do meet one, you can either **Host** them for a one-time engagement (a funded lecture, a building consultation, a hired performance) without any lasting commitment, or **Recruit** them outright into your household, converting them into a permanent companion or court appointment.

The sharpest part of this system: a Wanderer worth having is worth having to more than just you. A rival house can just as easily host or recruit the very person you've been planning to meet, and the race is resolved the instant either side actually commits — hesitate too long after hearing the rumor, and you may arrive to find the philosopher already gone, or worse, already settled into a rival's own household.

## Two hazard layers: disaster and disease

The world can turn on you suddenly, and it does so through two related but genuinely separate systems.

**Natural Disasters** cover fire, flood, earthquake, drought, storm, landslide, blight and infestation, frost, and — rare and set apart from the rest — volcanic eruption. Each carries its own **Exposure** reading, driven by your own choices: dense urban building raises fire risk, river-adjacent land raises flood risk, aggressive quarrying and clear-cutting raise landslide risk, and so on. This isn't a moral judgment on any particular choice — pushing your land harder for more output is a legitimate strategy, it just means accepting the exposure that comes with it. Hazards also interact: a dry season raises both drought and fire risk together, and a severe storm can trigger a flood on the same roll. Only earthquake carries no real countermeasure at all — the best you can do there is prepare for the aftermath, not prevent the event.

**Disease & Public Health** is a distinct layer, split into two tracks. **Endemic illness** is a chronic, ever-present background drain specific to your terrain and circumstances — malaria-like "Roman fever" in marshy ground, dysentery from poor sanitation, even diseases of wealth like gout from a rich diet, or lead poisoning from luxury cookware and wine. **Epidemics** are the acute, contagious version — real outbreaks that spread person to person or, in one case, through contaminated water, that you can try to contain through quarantine, though quarantine works far better against an ordinary local outbreak than against something moving empire-wide. A household that survives a serious epidemic gains real, lasting immunity to that specific disease — useful nursing expertise for the next time it comes around. A recurring **Sanitation Investment** policy is your one direct lever over the whole disease picture at once.

The two systems talk to each other in one deliberate way: disease doesn't get its own weather-style hazard rolls, but a bad flood or famine's crowding and scarcity is exactly the kind of condition that can tip a household into a genuine disease outbreak. Reading a disaster's aftermath as a warning sign for what might follow is part of managing both systems well.

## Graffiti and rumors

Your settlement's walls carry real, dynamically generated writing — reflecting whatever is actually going on in the world around it. An active scandal generates hostile graffiti naming the household involved; a contested election generates competing endorsements from both sides; a beloved gladiator or racing faction draws genuine fan commentary; even a well-regarded published work can earn admiring scrawl. You can "read the walls" at the Forum, a tavern, or the public baths as a light way of picking up ambient information, and — for a price — hire a professional letterer to paint favorable notices of your own, though the same letterer has no particular loyalty and can just as easily be hired by a rival, and hiring the same one repeatedly leaves a trail.

You can also go on the offensive: commissioning deliberately damaging graffiti about a rival is a real scheme in its own right, with real risk of being discovered and real risk of **backfiring** — a message aimed at someone who's currently well-liked, or one that's too obviously vicious, can end up damaging your own reputation more than the intended target's. Existing damaging graffiti can be paid off and erased, though erasure doesn't touch whatever underlying scandal or feud is actually generating it — if the root cause is still live, new writing simply reappears. Occasionally a single piece of graffiti is witty or significant enough to become genuinely **Famous** — remembered and quoted long after everyone involved has moved on or moved away.

## The City Bulletin: how you actually find out what happened

The **Monthly Bulletin** is your household's real news digest — not a new source of information, but the readable presentation layer sitting on top of everything else the game already tracks. It's organized into distinct sections (household affairs, economic summary, political news, public notices, social and scandal, games results, military and frontier news, weather and omens, the wider world, and highlights pulled straight from your Chronicle), and the single biggest story of the month is promoted to a genuine **Headline** leading the report, the way an actual newspaper's front page would. For the rare month with something genuinely enormous happening — a triumph, a succession, a war's conclusion — the Bulletin can break its usual structure entirely into a **Special Edition** built around that one event.

If you'd like the flavor of it, the same information can also be read as the **Daily Acta** — the same month's news broken apart into small, dated daily notices in the old Roman style, purely cosmetic dressing over the same underlying content.

What actually reaches you depends on who you are. A modest household never sees genuine imperial and cross-provincial news at all — not because it's hidden, but because it genuinely doesn't reach a household of that standing; a household that's climbed high enough in politics earns access to a further tier of far-reaching news on top of the ordinary local report. And not everything reported turns out to be true: a rumor that overstated a scandal, or a false report about a rival's fortunes, can later be walked back with an explicit **Correction**, sometimes with a real reputational cost landing on whoever spread the false story in the first place. The Bulletin itself is deliberately temporary — recent months stay fully readable in detail, but older ones fade down to just their headline and Chronicle-worthy highlights, exactly like an old newspaper. Anything genuinely worth permanently remembering already lives in your Dynasty Chronicle instead.

## Celebrities and influential figures

Fame is a single, shared measure — the same 0-100 score described in the Games & Spectacle chapter, tracked on every character in the game, not a separate system for arena stars versus everyone else. What this chapter's concern widens is *where that fame can come from*: a gifted orator, a celebrated poet or writer, a wandering specialist building a reputation on the road, a war hero, a courtesan or famous beauty, a Greek-style athlete, a charismatic religious figure — all of these can become genuinely famous through channels that have nothing to do with the arena.

The genuinely interesting wrinkle is that fame and formal standing are not the same axis, and they don't always move together. A gladiator or actor can be adored by the crowd while carrying real legal disrepute among the respectable classes — famous and disreputable at once, and that's not a contradiction, it's often the exact same social fact seen from two directions. A quiet, respected magistrate can be the reverse: well-regarded politically while being entirely unknown to the ordinary crowd. And the rare individual who manages both — a general who returns to popular acclaim and elite respect simultaneously — is about as good as it gets. A household that lets one of its own members chase fame through a disreputable path (the arena, the stage) is making a real, weighed bet: real popular leverage, at a real formal-standing cost among the people who matter politically. And fame is genuinely volatile — it can collapse suddenly, not just fade, when a celebrated figure dies, disgraces themselves, or falls from favor.

## Dynasty Chronicle and the Dynasty Legacy Tree

Your household keeps a real, readable historical record — the **Dynasty Chronicle** — written in the voice of an actual household annal rather than a system log. Every entry carries a short prose line, a category (births and deaths, marriages, politics, war, wealth and building, faith and scandal), a significance tier, and links to whoever it's actually about. Entries are tiered from Minor (logged but filtered out of your default view) up through Notable, Major, and Legendary — the tier that gets the richest illustrated treatment, reserved for a triumph, a dynasty's founding or extinction, or the battlefield death of someone who really mattered. You can read the Chronicle two ways at once: by generation, as a chapter for each head of household's tenure, or by category, cutting straight across every generation at once — "show me every war this family has ever fought." You can also set aspirational **Milestones** ahead of time — reach the consulship, found a second settlement, survive ten generations — which sit in the Chronicle as a pending goal until the day they're actually achieved. And it's yours to shape a little: you can pin any entry to always show up regardless of its assigned tier, and add your own free-text notes and pure diary entries alongside the generated record.

A separate, related system is the **Dynasty Legacy Tree** — a permanent, purchasable set of household-wide advantages, spent using **Memoria**, the same currency your household earns from properly honoring its dead. Where your household's doctrine emerges for free from how you've been playing and can fade if you drift away from it, a Legacy node is the opposite: deliberately bought once, and never lost afterward (unless your line goes fully extinct, in which case every unlocked Legacy is lost along with it). The tree has seven branches — blood and lineage, name and renown, household and court, inheritance and estate, ancestral valor, the ancestor cult, and learning and culture — each building through two ordinary tiers before ending in a genuine two-way fork with no obviously "correct" choice. Because Memoria can also be banked toward earning your family a lasting honorific name, spending it down on a Legacy node puts you in real, honest tension with that other long-term reward — there's no way to have everything at once.

## Organic and autonomous growth

All of the systems above share one deeper commitment: the world keeps developing on its own, whether or not you're engaging with it. Every month, after your own household's turn resolves, the wider world moves through its own fixed sequence — other households aging, marrying, and dying; settlements growing or shrinking; businesses forming and failing; politics and feuds playing out; culture and technology drifting forward; and, finally, any house that's gone extinct being replaced by a new one rising to take its place. Underneath all of it sits one shared mechanism, the **Growth Roll**: a simple three-way outcome (Growth, Stagnation, or Decline) weighted by whatever a given house, settlement, or trend is already trending toward, with real setbacks and real successes rather than everything smoothly improving forever. A rival house's own attempt to arrange a marriage, survive a succession, or grow a business can genuinely fail as easily as it can succeed — the same honest, sometimes-it-doesn't-work texture that makes the wider world feel like it's actually alive rather than merely decorative.

Most of this never produces anything you'll actually see — and that silence is intentional. The whole point is a world that keeps moving without demanding you watch every roll of it happen. What does reach you comes through the very systems already covered in this chapter: your Dynasty Chronicle catching anything that becomes genuinely significant, your correspondence refreshing a quiet rival's stale dossier, and your Monthly Bulletin gathering it all into something you can actually sit down and read.
