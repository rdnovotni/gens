# Estate & Economy

Everything in *Gens* ultimately runs on land, labor, and coin. This chapter is the long one: how your holding physically grows from a single villa into a town, what a room in your own house actually gets you, how raw materials become the goods you sell, who works your fields and how you treat them, where your money comes from and where it leaks away, and how all of that connects outward into a living regional economy — markets, rival merchants, shipping lanes, public works, and a wider population that is buying as much as it's working.

None of this is a single ledger you babysit line by line. Almost everything here runs automatically, month by month, off standing decisions you've already made — which buildings you built, which policies you set, who you put in charge. Your real job is reading the Monthly Report and adjusting course, not approving every transaction by hand.

## 1. Growing Your Land — Estate & Settlement

Your estate starts as a single villa on a single plot, and the land around it is the physical growth engine for everything else you'll do. The map isn't an abstract slot grid — it's real terrain, presented cleanly as legible tiles and icons rather than a simulated landscape: fertile plains for ordinary farming, hills for quarries and mines, forest for timber, coastline for ports and fishing, riverbank for mills and irrigation, and marsh or poor land that's cheap to acquire but yields little and invites disease. Every plot's terrain is fixed the moment the map is generated, and it gates what you can build there — you can't sink a mineshaft into a wheat field.

Building falls into six categories. **Agriculture**, **Industry**, and **Commerce** run genuinely long production chains — three or four tiers deep — because those are the categories where real depth belongs. **Civic**, **Military**, and **Monuments** stay shorter and more singular: a Forum, a Basilica, a Temple, or a Statue is a milestone you build once, not a yield choice like a fourth field would be. Civic buildings are also the one category that's public rather than private — once your settlement has a real population beyond your own household, a Bathhouse or a Temple benefits everyone living there, not just your family, while everything else you build stays entirely your household's own business.

Small, cheap buildings — a field, a shrine, a market stall — go up almost instantly, though they carry a short delay before they're producing at full strength. Bigger projects — a temple, an aqueduct, a Basilica — take months, draw ongoing money and labor for their whole construction, and simply take longer if you leave them under-resourced rather than failing outright. Every completed building also carries ongoing upkeep, and neglect (or unrepaired disaster damage) degrades its output over time until you deliberately repair it. You can demolish anything to reclaim the plot, at a real but partial cost — except a Monument, where tearing down a Statue or a Family Tomb carries a genuine reputational penalty, since erasing something built for legacy is a visible act.

You can commit your estate to a real economic identity — **Agrarian**, **Mercantile**, **Industrial**, or **Martial** — and specializing heavily rewards you with a compounding bonus the more your buildings lean one way. Diversifying forgoes that bonus but carries no penalty of its own: it's simply the steadier path, never a trap. Your starting region shapes this too, through both fixed bonuses (frontier land is cheap but riskier; the Greek East favors schools) and hard gates — a mine needs hills and mineral wealth in the region at once, a port needs a coast.

Land itself can be acquired four ways: bought outright with denarii, granted as a political favor, seized through conquest, or arriving through a marriage dowry or inheritance. Most expansion is simply paced by what you can afford, but a handful of especially valuable plots — a fertile field, anything coastal or river-adjacent — can be actively contested by a rival house wanting the same ground, turning that one acquisition into a real race rather than a guaranteed purchase.

### From villa to city

Your settlement advances through four real stages — **Villa → Vicus → Town → City** — and each stage needs both a population threshold and specific construction: a Vicus needs at least a market and a shrine; a Town needs a completed Forum; a City needs a completed Basilica and an Aqueduct. Hitting both thresholds together doesn't auto-advance you silently — the transition is a deliberate action you choose to take, and it's a genuine milestone, recorded in your Chronicle and felt as a real moment of arrival rather than a counter ticking over in the background.

At sufficient scale, a second settlement becomes possible, most naturally by absorbing a rival house's holdings through marriage, legal ruling, conquest, or their extinction — a late-game possibility, not a core part of early play. For the full mechanics behind land, terrain gating, and settlement growth, see [Estate & Settlement](../design/gens-estate-settlement-design.md).

## 2. Your Home — The Villa

Your estate's other buildings are production and civic infrastructure. The Villa is different: it's where you and your family actually live, day to day, and it gets its own dedicated interior view — a literal architectural cutaway you zoom into, room by room, the way a much smaller game might let you decorate a single apartment. The Villa has its own growth track, separate from your settlement's own Villa→Vicus→Town→City progression, tracking your household's personal wealth and taste rather than the town's population. It runs through three stages — **Villa Rustica**, the modest functional starting core; **Villa Urbana**, adding real leisure, ceremony, and self-sufficiency rooms as the family prospers; and **Domus/Palace**, the full elite household with dozens of rooms and a genuinely large staff to match.

A single **Grandeur Score** aggregates every room's tier and decoration into one tracked figure, feeding your household's prestige and gating whether you can even advance to the next Villa stage — a stage upgrade needs both the money and a household that's actually lived up to it room by room, not just paid for the privilege.

The room list is long and deliberately specific: an **Atrium** where clients call on you each morning, a **Tablinum** that's your actual home office, a **Triclinium** for formal dinners, a private **Lararium** for your household gods, an **Ergastulum** for the harshest labor housing, a **Cryptoporticus** for anything you'd rather keep quiet. Every family member, freedman, client, or companion with their own **Cubiculum** can have it personalized, drawing on their actual traits and appearance rather than a disconnected decoration minigame — a studious person's room defaults toward books, a resentful slave's assigned space reads as deliberately bare regardless of budget. Decoration itself runs on real historical style families rather than a generic "opulent" slider: the four Pompeian fresco styles for the Italian heartland, Provincial Gallic woodwork and Celtic motifs for the frontier, Punic-Iberian floor mosaics, Hellenistic marble and sculpture for the Greek East, or a cosmopolitan Provincial Fusion for a family that's traveled widely and doesn't want to commit to one tradition.

As your surrounding settlement grows from Vicus toward City, your Villa feels it both ways: better goods access, cheaper construction, and stronger security on one hand, but real noise, crowding, and lost privacy on the other — a countryside villa and a townhouse swallowed by its own city's growth are genuinely different propositions, not a strictly upward curve. Staffing your household is its own layer too: a handful of powerful Senior Positions (a Steward, a Bodyguard, a household Physician) scale up with your Villa's stage, alongside dozens of ordinary named staff roles for anyone running a specific room. See [The Villa](../design/gens-villa-design.md) for the complete room list, staffing roster, and decoration system.

## 3. Buildings & Production Chains

Beneath the categories above sits a real production economy: almost nothing is a single building. Bread needs a wheat field *and* a mill *and* a bakery. A purple-trimmed toga needs a flax field, a linen works, a weaver's loom, a murex-hunting operation, a dye works, *and* a tailoring house. Every chain is built from an authentic Roman industry, and every chain now names the actual good it produces rather than an abstract yield number — so your storehouses actually fill up with Grain, Wine, Textiles, or Tools you can see and sell.

A few concrete examples of how raw materials become value:

- **Grain → Bread.** A Wheat Field produces Grain; a Grain Mill turns it into Flour; a Bakery turns that into Bread. Simple, short, and the backbone of every settlement's daily life.
- **Wool → Textiles.** A Pasture's sheep yield Wool; a Weaver's Loom turns it into Cloth; a Tailoring House turns that into Tunics — or, paired with a coastal murex harvest and a Dye Works producing genuine Tyrian Purple, into a Purple-Trimmed Toga, one of the longest and most prestigious chains in the game.
- **Iron → Tools.** An Iron Mine (hills only) plus a Charcoal Kiln feeds an Ironworks, which turns raw Iron into either Weapons and Armor at an Armory, or ordinary Tools at a Smithy.

Not every building makes a good at all — a Tavern, a Brothel, a Slave Market, and a Banker's office (the Argentaria) generate income or an effect directly as **services**, with no chain behind them, played with the same frankness the rest of the economy is. Buildings also carry a light functional tag — Production, Service, Prestige, Governance, Housing, or Defense — that's just a reading aid, not a rigid classification: a Library, for instance, is genuinely both a functional Education benefit and a prestige one at once.

Livestock gets its own lighter treatment: rather than a flat trickle of Wool or Milk, every pasture-type building tracks a real herd headcount that breeds, is culled, and can be lost to disease, disaster, or rustling, governed by a standing Herd Strategy — Growth-Focused, Balanced, or Yield-Maximizing — rather than a decision you make every month. For the complete building roster, every showcase chain, and the full terrain-gating table, see [Buildings & Production Chains](../design/gens-buildings-design.md).

## 4. Goods

Every material your estate produces, stores, ages, or sells lives in one unified registry of **144 goods** — everything from raw Grain and Iron Ore through intermediate goods like Flour and Charcoal, finished goods like Bread and Furniture, luxury goods like Perfume and Jewelry, up to a small handful of **Imported Goods** — Silk, Eastern Spices, Baltic Amber — that never have a domestic production chain at all, no matter how much you invest; they can only be bought, and only once you've built a Port or a well-developed Emporium.

Storage is specific to the good, not generic: grain goes into a Granary, wine and oil age in an Apotheca, precious metals and jewelry sit in a Strongroom, and most everyday goods go into an ordinary Warehouse. Perishability matters just as concretely — some goods (Milk, Fish, fresh Bread) spoil within days if unused; others (Grain, Cheese, Medicine) decay gradually over a longer buffer; and true non-perishables (metal, stone, pottery, tools) never spoil at all. Wine is the notable exception that runs backward: stored properly in an Apotheca, it actually *improves* with age rather than decaying. A standing Preservation Policy can even convert fruit and grapes automatically into non-perishable dried fruit and raisins before they're lost.

Many goods also carry a **Quality tier** — Common, Fine, or Exceptional — set by the quality of your inputs and the skill of whoever's running the building, and Quality is what actually makes a good worth gifting, displaying, or commanding a premium price rather than only being sold wholesale. Livestock headcounts, by contrast, never carry Quality at all — a herd doesn't have individual craftsmanship the way a bolt of cloth does. Every good's price responds to the shared regional market described below, moving with supply, demand, season, and disruption rather than sitting at a fixed number. See [Resources & Goods](../design/gens-resources-goods-design.md) for the full registry, including regional specialties and every good's storage and spoilage rule.

## 5. Labor & Slavery

Your buildings need people to run them, and *Gens* treats how you staff and treat those people as one of its most serious systems. Labor arrives through genuinely different doors: bought at a slave market, captured in war (cheaper, but arriving with worse loyalty and a real chance of resentment), bonded into service over unpaid debt, born to an enslaved mother, inherited or gifted, seized through legal proceedings, or bought from a pirate or bandit at real reputational risk if the connection ever becomes known. A person's full capability is never fully visible at the point of purchase — you see an apparent age, a rough health range, and an origin, while exact skills, personality, and hidden injuries stay uncertain until you actually own them, and a seller can genuinely lie about what you're buying.

Day-to-day treatment runs on a standing **Regimen** — four simple, tiered settings covering Diet, Accommodation, Permitted Freedoms, and Discipline Strictness — that you can set for a whole group ("all Field Hands") and then override individually for anyone who warrants different treatment, like a trusted steward given real comforts while the rest of the field gang stays on a harsher default. There's no dominant setting: better treatment costs more and caps short-term output lower, but builds loyalty and suppresses unrest over time; harsher treatment raises the output ceiling and cuts costs now, at a rising risk of unrest, flight, and lasting resentment the longer it's sustained.

Beyond that standing policy sits a separate, blunter **Punishment** ladder for one-off discipline — from extra duties at the mild end, through corporal punishment, up to maiming and, rarely, execution — and every rung logs to your Chronicle and carries real, escalating consequences of its own, including a chance of drawing actual legal scrutiny for excessive cruelty. **Flight** is a real mechanic, not an abstraction: low loyalty, harsh treatment, and certain traits raise the odds someone runs, usually during travel, a disaster, or simply an unwatched moment, and once someone's fled you have a limited window to hire bounty hunters or send a household officer after them before the trail goes cold — recapture, permanent loss, or worse are all real outcomes.

**Manumission** — freeing a slave — is a real choice with real consequences, not a button you press once you feel like it. Roman law gave you three routes: a formal act before a magistrate (moderate cost, immediate, and a small reputational credit for doing it properly), a grant in your will (free now, but only takes effect on your death), or enrollment during a periodic census. However it happens, the person becomes a freedman, takes your family name, and typically keeps serving you in some capacity — often the very same duty, now as paid labor, or even promoted into a real position of household trust — carrying ongoing obligations to you as their patron rather than simply walking away. Selling someone, by contrast, is handled with the same frankness: the game will surface a family bond or a couple's relationship plainly before a sale confirms rather than softening it, and breaking one apart has real, felt consequences among those left behind. All of this sits behind a content-intensity toggle that softens the narration without ever changing the underlying mechanics. See [Labor, Slavery & Punishment](../design/gens-labor-slavery-design.md) for the complete acquisition, Regimen, and manumission rules.

## 6. Money — Economy & Finance

Every good your estate produces ultimately turns into denarii, and your household's finances are tracked in a single **Treasury** balance that can run negative — which matters, because debt is a real, load-bearing mechanic rather than decoration. Income comes from more than just goods sold: rent from tenant farmers and urban tenants living on your land, one-off or standing supply contracts, tax revenue if you hold the right office, and genuine windfalls — war spoils, a received dowry, an inheritance, a lucky find. Expenses run wider than upkeep too: wages for any hired rather than enslaved staff, deliberate bribes spent against a specific ruling or negotiation, funded actions that buy prestige or favor outright (games, festivals, public works), and capital expenditure — the big one-off purchases like a slave-market acquisition or a Villa upgrade — that's the category most likely to push you into debt in a single stroke.

**Taxation runs both directions.** Outbound, you pay tribute to Rome or the provincial administration regardless of how you feel about it, and missing a payment damages your standing specifically with Rome, distinct from your local reputation. Inbound, if you hold the right local office, you can set your own tax policy on your settlement — an indirect toll on trade, or a direct levy on harvests and rent — and either setting is a real tradeoff: more income now, at the cost of contentment and a rising risk that people simply leave.

**Debt cuts both ways too.** Borrow, and a sustained default escalates through rising interest, real legal exposure, seizure of your own assets, and — the sharpest, rarest outcome — a free household member of lesser legal status being bonded into debt slavery to satisfy what you owe. Lend, and the same ladder runs in reverse: a defaulted debtor's assets, and eventually the debtor themselves, can become yours. A **maritime loan** (*fenus nauticum*) is the era's closest thing to marine insurance — you finance a risky sea voyage at a steep premium, and if the ship is genuinely lost, the debt is simply forgiven rather than triggering default.

Everything above tracks money *moving*. A separate figure, **Net Worth**, tracks what your household is actually *worth* at any moment — treasury balance, stored goods, livestock, land and buildings, minus what you owe — and it's read, not spent: it feeds marriage negotiations, comparisons against rival houses, and inheritance, without ever becoming a second currency you can draw on directly. And if your Net Worth stays deeply negative for too long, your household can slide into **Insolvency** — forced liquidation of your goods, forced sale of your own plots, even your Villa's own stage being pushed backward — a real, painful state, but never a game over: every lever that got you there remains available to claw your way back out.

All of this sits on top of a shared **regional market** that behaves like a real economy rather than a fixed price list: supply and demand shift with the seasons, and a disaster, an outbreak of piracy, or a war can genuinely disrupt trade — the same market every rival house in the region is also drawing from and selling into. See [Economy & Finance](../design/gens-economy-finance-design.md) for the full ledger, tax, and debt mechanics.

## 7. Property Beyond the Estate — Land Ownership & Real Estate

Estate & Settlement covers the land you personally build on and farm. Beyond that, your household's wealth can also become a real, named **property portfolio** — specific villas, insula blocks, workshops, warehouses, farms, and ships, each individually ownable, leasable, buyable, and sellable, sitting alongside your core estate rather than replacing it. Ownership itself is wider than just your household: a rival gens, an individual freedman shopkeeper, a temple, a trade guild, the Roman state itself (which only ever leases public land, never sells it), or even the Emperor's own personal holdings can all hold property you might one day acquire, inherit into, or buy out.

Cities are divided into a handful of named **Districts** — a Forum District, a Warehouse District, an Elite Quarter — each with its own property-value trend driven by population, disasters, and nearby monuments, giving simple ownership real texture: an identical building reads very differently in the Elite Quarter than in the Warehouse District.

You don't have to run everything yourself. Any developed property can be flagged **Leased Out** rather than directly managed, handed to a named Operator — a companion, a client, a freedman — whose own loyalty determines how the arrangement actually goes: a steady operator remits reliable income and frees your attention; a skimming one quietly under-reports what they collect; and an ambitious, successful one can eventually save enough to buy the property outright, becoming an independent owner and the seed of their own future family line. A **Societas** — a real, distinct legal partnership between two or more owners — is how multiple households pool capital for a shared venture, most classically a shipping voyage, and a **Publicanus Contract** lets a household of the right standing bid to collect a province's taxes on Rome's behalf, keeping the difference between what's actually collected and what's owed.

There's no hard cap on how large your portfolio can grow, but every property beyond a certain point adds real administrative burden — you offset it the same way you offset any other management problem, by hiring more overseers or delegating through a steward, rather than personally attending to everything at once. See [Land Ownership, Estates & the Real Estate Market](../design/gens-land-ownership-real-estate-design.md) for the full ownership, leasing, and district-value rules.

## 8. Competing in Business

Roman commercial life ran on two opposite instincts at once — cooperation among trades and ruthless rivalry between named businesses — and *Gens* builds both.

**Notable Businesses** gives a real, specific rival a face: a "Bakery of Marcus Livius" carries its own Reputation, distinct from its owner's personal standing, a named main competitor, a named supplier whose own bad harvest or bankruptcy can disrupt your supply chain, and real behaviors like merging with a rival, specializing into one high-quality good, moving to a wealthier district, or lobbying for a government contract.

**Business Competition** is the full escalation ladder between two such rivals: ordinary rivalry, a deliberate price war, predatory pricing sustained past the point of ordinary profit specifically to bankrupt the other side, and finally forced consolidation — where the winner becomes the single most natural buyer of the very business it just broke. Undercutting an outside rival is simply business; undercutting a fellow member of your own trade guild is a real betrayal, carrying its own reputational cost within that guild and a genuine risk of collective retaliation. Two businesses can also choose the cooperative path instead — a cartel fixing prices or dividing customers by district — which is more stable but always fragile, since any participant's own ambition or greed is a standing temptation to secretly break the deal. Grain hoarding during a genuine shortage is treated as this economy's single most severe form of misconduct, carrying real risk of mob violence and formal prosecution rather than an ordinary market outcome.

**Merchant Families & the Equestrian Order** is the social layer sitting above all of this: the real, formally recognized *Ordo Equester*, a genuine census-qualified class between ordinary citizens and the Senate, with its own visible marker (a narrow purple stripe on the toga), its own reserved public seating, and its own exclusive offices. A merchant family can be genuinely wealthier than an old senatorial house while carrying far less inherited prestige — the reverse of a declining aristocratic house's own "prestige outliving fortune" problem — and closing that gap means deliberately purchasing respectability: funding games or public works, marrying into an old but cash-poor house, or holding a local magistracy as a visible stepping stone.

**Societates & Business Partnerships** goes deeper still into what forming a partnership actually means: real, distinct Roman partnership types (a narrow one-voyage partnership versus a rare, all-in pooling of an entire fortune), real governance models (equal partners, a dominant partner, or a purely silent investor), and the single most dramatic fact this system surfaces — a partner's liability was never limited to what they invested. If the partnership failed, your entire personal fortune was exposed. See [Business Competition](../design/gens-business-competition-design.md), [Merchant Families & the Equestrian Order](../design/gens-merchant-families-design.md), [Notable Businesses](../design/gens-notable-businesses-design.md), and [Societates & Business Partnerships](../design/gens-societates-business-partnerships-design.md) for the full mechanics.

## 9. Infrastructure & Public Works

Beyond your production buildings, four related systems cover the connective tissue of your estate and your civic life.

**Private Infrastructure** is what actually links your own plots together: a Dirt Track is free but does nothing; a proper Paved Road speeds goods and Commerce between connected plots, and three or more plots joined that way form a Road Cluster carrying a real, aggregate efficiency bonus. A well-connected estate is a genuine double edge, though — a road that speeds your own carts also speeds a rival's raiders, so a household investing heavily in roads without a matching look at boundary walls and security posture is making a real, legible bet. Beyond roads, this is also where you formalize irrigation (or a cheaper well/cistern alternative), reclaim marsh and poor land into better terrain, and build private bridges and boundary walls.

**Public Works & Euergetism** is the real Roman institution of civic obligation: a wealthy household isn't merely free to fund the settlement's aqueducts, roads, sewers, marketplaces, and harbors — genuine social expectation held that you *owed* the community a visible contribution, and conspicuously never providing one carries a quiet, ongoing reputational cost of its own. A funded public work commonly bears a formal inscription crediting you by name, a real path toward earning a lasting title, and rival households genuinely competed to out-fund each other's civic generosity — a real "arms race," but one where the whole settlement benefits rather than either side getting hurt.

**Public Contracts & Competitive Bidding** is how the state's own business actually gets let out to bid, under a real magistracy — the **Censor** — who also runs the periodic five-year census reassessing everyone's wealth. Three real contract types exist: tax-farming (*Publicani*), public-works construction (a *Redemptor* bidding to actually build what a patron funds), and military or provincial supply contracts. Every bid is judged on price, reliability, and raw political influence — and a contractor who cuts corners to pad their own margin risks a real fraud prosecution and permanent disqualification if caught, echoing a genuine historical scandal from the Second Punic War.

**Public Auctions** is the *auctio* — the ordinary, everyday institution for competitively selling goods, property, or people at public sale, run by a hired auctioneer rather than a magistrate. A single estate liquidation can run many lots in one day, and early lots create real momentum — win one at a high price and later lots draw sharper competing bids, rewarding a patient bidder who lets others show their hand first. You can bid in person or send an agent with fixed instructions if you can't attend, and two genuinely dishonest options exist on both sides of the gavel: a **Ring** of buyers secretly agreeing not to bid against each other, and **Puffing** — a seller planting a shill bidder to inflate the price — both real schemes carrying real discovery risk and real legal consequences. See [Private Infrastructure](../design/gens-private-infrastructure-design.md), [Public Works & Euergetism](../design/gens-public-works-euergetism-design.md), [Public Contracts & Competitive Bidding](../design/gens-public-contracts-competitive-bidding-design.md), and [Public Auctions](../design/gens-public-auctions-design.md) for full mechanics.

## 10. Trade Routes and Shipping

Trade in *Gens* mostly runs passively in the background, but two systems give it real, ownable substance.

**Private Ships & Shipping Ventures** treats an individual merchant vessel the way your Villa treats a room, or Land Ownership treats a property: a real, named, persistent asset you commission, age, insure, lose, and sometimes mourn. The registry spans real historical vessel types — the sturdy, round-hulled *Corbita* that makes up most ordinary Marines, the enormous Alexandrian grain carriers, fast *Liburnian* dispatch galleys, horse-transport ships, river ferries, and even a purely ornamental pleasure barge with no cargo capacity at all, built to be seen rather than to earn a return. You can commission a ship to order — choosing its hull class, build quality, and decoration — and give it a formal religious consecration at launch for a small, permanent reduction in future misfortune. A household with more than one ship can name a **Flagship**, its premier vessel: real prestige simply by existing, the natural choice for a family member traveling by sea or hosting a diplomatic meeting aboard, but correspondingly heavier stakes — a guaranteed entry in your Chronicle — if it's ever lost. Most routine voyages resolve as background averages, but a genuinely high-stakes trip — an expensive luxury cargo, a ship financed by a maritime loan, or your own Flagship regardless of cargo — resolves as a real, discrete event: arrived safely, damaged, lost to storm, lost to piracy, captured, or simply never seen again.

**Named Roads & Trade Itineraries** gives the abstract distances between settlements real, historically specific infrastructure. The Via Appia, the Via Egnatia, the Via Domitia, and other real arteries physically tie the game's regions together, and a household can sponsor and maintain a specific stretch of one under the real office of *Curator Viarum*, collecting standing toll income and measurably speeding travel and correspondence along it. You can build and operate waystations — an overnight inn (a *Mansio*) or a horse-changing post (a *Mutatio*) — as real income-generating property, and define a named **Trade Itinerary**, a planned multi-leg route that runs measurably faster and safer if you've actually invested in the road and waystations along it. The rarest, highest-prestige act in this whole system is funding an entirely new road extension into previously unconnected territory — a genuinely permanent mark on the map, carrying your family's name the way the real Via Appia still carries its builder's, over two thousand years later. See [Private Ships & Shipping Ventures](../design/gens-private-ships-shipping-ventures-design.md) and [Named Roads & Trade Itineraries](../design/gens-named-roads-trade-itineraries-design.md) for the full detail.

## 11. Who Can Afford What — Population Wealth & Purchasing Power

The settlement's wider population isn't just where your labor and tenants come from — it's also a market with its own real buying power, and that buying power is nothing like a modern consumer economy. The ancient economy was a steep pyramid: the overwhelming majority of people lived at or near real subsistence, with almost all discretionary spending concentrated in a genuinely narrow elite tier. *Gens* builds that pyramid directly into its existing population groups rather than assuming everyone has some money to spend: tenant farmers and laborers sit at **Subsistence**, spending almost entirely on food and shelter, where a price spike in basic grain isn't an inconvenience but a genuine crisis; artisans and shopkeepers in ordinary standing sit at **Modest Surplus**, with real but thin discretionary spending that's the first thing cut when times turn hard; and the settlement's local notables and its most prosperous households sit at **Elite Discretionary** — the narrow tier where almost all of your luxury-goods and imported-goods demand actually lives.

This has a direct, practical consequence for any business you or a rival runs: a bakery selling ordinary bread can thrive almost anywhere real population exists at all, because the demand is thin per person but universal. A perfumer or a fine-jewelry workshop needs a genuine elite population nearby to survive, regardless of the quality of what it makes — which is exactly why a struggling luxury business's real fix is often relocating to a wealthier district rather than simply making a better product. Purchasing power also isn't uniform across your map: a dense, wealthy region reads a much larger elite tier than a raw frontier settlement, and even within one city, an Elite Quarter and a Warehouse District carry very different real spending power despite sharing the same city walls. Basic goods carry real political weight no luxury good ever does — a grain price spike is a direct threat to contentment and a genuine trigger for unrest, in a way a spike in perfume prices never could be. See [Population Wealth & Purchasing Power](../design/gens-population-wealth-purchasing-power-design.md) for the full model, and [Settlement Demographics](../design/gens-settlement-demographics-design.md) for how the underlying population itself grows, migrates, and moves between classes in the first place.
