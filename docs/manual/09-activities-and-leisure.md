# Activities and Leisure

Not everything your household does is a spreadsheet decision. A feast, a hunt, a pilgrimage, a wedding — these are events with a beginning, a middle, and an end, where the people you've gathered together actually matter, and where something can genuinely go right or wrong in the moment. This chapter covers how the game handles a deliberately convened occasion, the specific kinds of occasions built on top of that shared structure, and how you and your household actually get from one place to another in the first place.

## The Activity Engine: one shared shape for every gathering

Underneath a Feast, a Hunt, a Wedding, or a Pilgrimage sits the same underlying machinery — the **Activity Engine**. It's worth understanding once, because everything else in this chapter is simply a specific flavor of it.

The game already has two other ways for people to interact: a quick, routine **Group Interaction** — an ordinary morning of callers, a plain family dinner — resolved in a single tick with no real internal structure, and a **Scheme** — a covert, adversarial plot working against a specific target's awareness. An **Activity** is a genuine third thing: a deliberately convened, named gathering with a real guest list, a real host, and real internal stages, where the stakes are higher precisely because a defined set of people has actually been brought together on purpose.

Every Activity, regardless of type, is built from the same six pieces:

1. **Host** — whoever convened it. This doesn't have to be you; a rival house or a notable family can host one entirely on their own initiative, and you can be invited — or, tellingly, pointedly left off the guest list.
2. **Type** — the specific kind of Activity (a Feast, a Hunt, and so on), each described later in this chapter.
3. **Venue** — a real, specific location: a room in your villa, a public civic space, or open wilderness for something like a Hunt. A finer venue genuinely improves how the Activity turns out.
4. **Guest List** — who's actually there, drawn from your family, your clients, your companions, or anyone else the host chooses to invite.
5. **Duration** — either **Quick** (resolved within a single month, like an ordinary Feast) or **Extended** (spanning real months, like a Wedding's full lead-up or a genuine wilderness expedition), and an Extended Activity can be interrupted by an unrelated crisis elsewhere in the world.
6. **Phases** — the actual stages the Activity moves through, from an opening reception through the main event to its aftermath.

Every guest invited is a real decision with real weight. Accepting or declining an invitation reads a character's existing relationship with the host — a rival mid-feud with you might decline outright, or might accept specifically looking to cause trouble. And leaving someone off a guest list they'd reasonably expect to be on is its own real insult, even though nothing was said or done — a snub is a genuine social signal the game tracks, not just an absence.

Two separate qualities describe how any Activity actually goes: **Scale** — how big, how public, how visible, set deliberately by the host from the guest list size and venue chosen — and **Quality** — how well the thing was actually executed, judging the specific inputs that Activity Type calls for. These are kept deliberately independent: a small, intimate gathering can be flawlessly executed, and a huge, lavish spectacle can still be badly run. Bigger is never automatically better.

Whatever happens during an Activity's phases — a proposal, a duel, a quiet aside, a rival pointedly snubbed — gets pulled together at the end into a single readable **Activity Record**, so you get one legible account of how the occasion actually went rather than having to piece it together from a dozen separate moments. And because a Guest List is a real, defined set of people, it doubles as the **Witness Pool** for anything risky that happens during the Activity — a scheme confronted mid-feast, a secret exposed at exactly the wrong social moment. A bigger, more lavish gathering cuts both ways: more prestige if it goes well, and a bigger audience for anything that goes wrong.

## Feasts

A Feast is the most straightforward Activity Type, and mostly a matter of wiring existing banquet mechanics — the ingredients, preparation, and venue quality that determine how good the food and hospitality actually are — into this shared structure. What the Activity framing adds on top is real internal shape: an Arrival & Seating phase, the Meal itself, a distinctly more informal after-dinner phase (the *comissatio*, a natural setting for a private word, a quiet proposal, or a scheme's next move), and Departure.

The single most interesting new piece is **seating**. A formal dining room's couches carry a real internal hierarchy, and where you seat someone is a genuine, deliberate signal: seating a guest below the standing they'd reasonably expect is a real insult even if they were properly invited and did attend, while seating them above their expected standing is a real, visible honor — one that risks a jealous reaction from whoever got bumped to make room. Managing a large or politically delicate guest list well means thinking about the seating chart as its own puzzle, not just deciding who gets an invitation.

A Feast also carries a **Purpose** — a patronage dinner, a funeral feast, a religious festival, a victory banquet, a competitive display of generosity, or simply an ordinary social occasion — which shapes what guests expect from it without changing the underlying mechanics. And a Feast that's ostentatiously showy runs a real risk of drawing unwanted official attention if a magistrate decides it's been held a little too visibly for the household's actual standing.

## Games & Spectacle

Hosting public games — gladiatorial combat, chariot racing, or theatrical performance — is one of the biggest patronage levers available to a household, far bigger than an ordinary funded civic project. Staging one means committing real money to a venue, choosing the format and the stakes, and fielding competitors, either drawn from a professional troupe or, for a genuine personal stake, your own household's people.

Gladiatorial combat is built around a real choice of stakes: an ordinary match expects mercy for the loser as the norm (though even there, a rowdy crowd's verdict can occasionally still go the other way), while a declared death match draws a bigger crowd and a bigger reputational swing at the cost of an asset you can't get back. Beast hunts pit a hunter against an exotic animal at real cost, since a beast lost in the arena is gone for good; executions by beast are handled as a distinct, harsher category with no sporting pretense attached, staged rather than played for entertainment. Group battles and the rare, spectacular mock naval battle (the *naumachia*) scale the same combat resolution up to a much larger and more dangerous affair. Chariot racing runs on the four historical color factions, each with its own local following, and resolves through a charioteer's skill and a team's horses. Theatrical performance is the one entirely non-violent format, judged on the lead performer's learning and skill and capable of landing anywhere from triumphant to scandalous, depending on how bold the content was and who was actually in the audience.

Every one of these generates **Fame** — a single, shared, universal reputation score any character can carry, described more fully in the Living World chapter's discussion of celebrities. This document is simply the richest single generator of that shared field, not a separate fame system of its own. A famous competitor is a genuine asset — bigger crowds, better odds, real prestige for the household that fields them — but also a genuine liability: a well-known champion is exactly the kind of target a jealous rival might pay a bandit confederation to kidnap or kill out of spite.

Audience wagering is a real mechanic in its own right: you can bet on an outcome before it resolves, with odds set by the competitors' own known standing and fame, and the house hosting the event takes a cut of every wager placed regardless of outcome.

## Hunts, Beast Taming & Menageries

A Hunt is a real Activity in its own right — a genuine social occasion with its own guest list, not merely a solitary pursuit. It moves through preparation, the pursuit itself, a kill-or-capture resolution, and an aftermath that's often a celebratory feast of its own. Game comes in four tiers: **Common** (boar, deer — low risk, modest reward), **Dangerous** (wolves, bears — real injury risk), **Exotic** (lions, elephants — found only on the frontier or abroad, usually requiring a hired specialist handler), and **Legendary**.

Legendary game is a deliberate, bounded exception to how grounded the rest of the game usually is: griffins, hydras, manticores, basilisks, and a handful of other genuine creatures out of Greco-Roman mythology are real, confirmed inhabitants of the world's own wild and unmapped edges — not rumor, not misidentified ordinary animals. You'll never stumble into this content by accident; it only matters to a household that deliberately goes looking for it, tied to specific, real locations already established elsewhere in the world (the Nemean Lion's wild countryside, the cave at Lake Avernus popularly believed to lead to the underworld, and so on). Facing one properly requires real preparation — the right gear, sometimes a specific counter to that creature's own signature danger — and an underprepared party risks a genuine, serious defeat rather than an automatic heroic win.

Every Hunt ends in one of two outcomes: a **Kill**, which yields ordinary goods and a trophy for display, or a **Capture**, which is harder to pull off but is the only path to something worth keeping alive. A captured animal isn't automatically tame — a specialist handler works with it over real time and at real risk before it's safe to keep. A particularly notable specimen — a rare species, a survivor of a great hunt, a gift from a foreign ruler — becomes a real, individually named animal with its own provenance and its own natural lifespan in your **Menagerie**, the collection room built for exactly this. An unmaintained or understaffed Menagerie carries a real risk of an animal simply escaping into the settlement — a serious incident for an ordinary exotic animal, and a genuine regional emergency if what got loose was legendary.

For what each of these creatures actually is, mythologically and otherwise, see the Bestiary in the [World Atlas](10-world-atlas.md) chapter — this chapter only covers hunting, taming, and keeping them.

## Pilgrimages

A Pilgrimage sends one specific member of your household on a real journey to a real, named holy site — Delphi, Eleusis, Ephesus, and several others already established elsewhere in the game's world — for a specific religious purpose. It's built directly on top of the Activity Engine and Travel's own journey mechanics rather than inventing a separate system: its "guest list" is really a small travel party, and its venue is wherever the destination actually is.

A Pilgrimage can serve several distinct purposes. You might **consult an oracle** for a genuine, unusually reliable prophecy about a specific looming decision. You might undertake a **mystery initiation**, a permanent, one-time rite that marks a household member with full membership in a cult. You might seek a **healing pilgrimage** at a renowned sanctuary for relief from a chronic condition, with real, honest uncertainty rather than a guaranteed cure. Or you might simply travel out of devotion to your household's patron deity, or as an act of penance following a scandal or run of bad omens.

One especially satisfying mechanic tied to this: a **vow**. In a genuine moment of crisis — a storm at sea, a battlefield emergency, a serious illness — a character can promise a god that, if spared, they'll undertake a specific pilgrimage in thanks. Once the crisis passes, that promise becomes a real, standing obligation; failing to fulfill it is a serious religious matter carrying a lasting penalty, while actually keeping the vow pays out a real reward on top of whatever the pilgrimage itself would normally grant.

None of this comes cheap or safe. The journey there and back runs through Travel's own ordinary risks in full — a Pilgrimage gets no special protection just because its purpose is religious, and the road doesn't care why someone is on it. Most households, most of the time, simply rely on their local household shrine and never make the trip at all; a Pilgrimage is meant to be an occasional, weighty choice, not a routine action.

## Social Places

Not every social encounter needs to be a hosted Activity. **Social Places** cover the far more ordinary experience of simply going out into a settlement's public spaces on any given day and running into people — the public baths, a barbershop (proverbially the best source of gossip in any Roman town), a tavern, the quick-service food counter known as a *thermopolium*, the forum, the covered market, an elite colonnade for an unhurried stroll, a gymnasium, the crowd around the arena, the harbor docks, even the public latrines. Visiting one is a quick, light action: a small, semi-random handful of people happen to be there, and you get a lighter, more casual slice of the ordinary conversation options — nothing as dramatic as what an Activity's phases unlock, but a real, low-commitment way to gather information or make a connection.

Venues come in **Common** and **Fine** tiers, tracking the same building-quality tiers used elsewhere — a finer venue skews toward more prominent company at the cost of being a less likely place to bump into an ordinary stranger or a wandering visitor. Social Places are also the one setting in the whole game where people who'd never plausibly meet in a private, curated setting can genuinely cross paths — a citizen and a freedman sharing the baths, a household slave and a noblewoman's wife both browsing the market. Visiting the same place regularly builds a real, if modest, standing reputation there. And a rougher venue carries a small, real risk of petty theft — nothing that makes visiting a trap, but a genuine safety tradeoff a finer, pricier venue avoids.

If a casual visit isn't quite enough, you can arrange a **Private Hire** — paying to reserve a bath session or a tavern's back room for your own occasion — which converts an ordinary visit into a genuine, small Activity in its own right, complete with a real host, a deliberate guest list, and access to higher-stakes conversation, without the cost and formality of hosting a full event at your villa.

## Travel

Travel is how you and your household actually get anywhere — Rome, a provincial capital, a rival's own estate, the frontier, an active military campaign, or a second settlement you hold elsewhere. Picking a destination and committing to it resolves as one block of travel time that scales with real distance — a short hop to Rome from an Italian estate, a much longer haul from a frontier posting — with a scattering of events along the way: some purely flavorful, some genuinely risky, weighted up sharply on dangerous routes like the frontier or open sea. Whoever you bring with you on the journey is your real protection: a bodyguard cuts down ambush risk, a physician reduces disease risk, exactly the retinue roles covered in the household chapter.

Every member of your family carries their own current location, not just you — a son can genuinely be off in Rome while you're managing the estate at home, and any number of household members can be traveling on entirely separate journeys at the same time, each resolving independently. This cuts both ways: sending someone essential off on their own errand leaves whatever post they held back home effectively unstaffed for as long as they're gone, the same real cost as taking them with you in person. And because destinations are real, persistent places, two people who happen to be there at the same time — your own traveling relative and a rival's, both in Rome on unrelated business — can genuinely run into each other, entirely outside anything you planned.

Arriving somewhere opens a curated menu specific to that destination — seeking a sponsor in Rome, the full range of personal interactions available at a rival's own estate, negotiating with a foreign power on the frontier, reviewing an appointed steward's work in person at a second settlement, or stepping directly into an unfolding campaign. A destination you've visited before picks up from where things stood last time rather than resetting, so a second visit to the same rival's estate is a continuation of that relationship, not a fresh start. And a trip isn't an unbreakable commitment — a genuine crisis back home can trigger a **Recall**, cutting the journey short and heading straight back, though at the real cost of forfeiting whatever you traveled there to accomplish.

Travel is also the only way to do certain things at all — anything genuinely requiring a body physically present, like a duel, an in-person legal hearing, or the later stages of a courtship. Its natural counterpart is **Correspondence**, covered in the Politics & Society chapter: a slower, safer, lower-stakes way of staying in touch with someone, negotiating from a distance, or simply hearing news, without ever having to leave home. Neither replaces the other — Correspondence is what you reach for when the stakes don't justify the risk and cost of the road, and Travel is what you reach for when they do.
