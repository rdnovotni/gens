# Getting Started

This chapter walks through founding your first household, the screens you'll live in day to day, and the rhythm the game runs on. If you haven't read the [Introduction](01-introduction.md) yet, it's worth a look first for the bigger picture of what kind of game this is.

## Starting a game

### Choosing a starting region

Before your household exists, you choose where it sits in the Roman world. The **starting region** you pick shapes your land quality, the local economy, the politics you'll be dealing with day one, and the threats your estate is exposed to — an Italian heartland start puts you close to Rome with steep political competition and expensive land; a frontier province gets you cheap land and room to expand at the cost of higher risk and lower starting prestige; a colonial or eastern start carries its own distinct mix of trade opportunity and local custom. The full roster of regions, and the detail behind each one, is covered in the [World Atlas](10-world-atlas.md) chapter — for now, just know that this choice colors nearly everything downstream of it.

Frontier starts carry one extra wrinkle worth knowing up front: a **Reputation Duality**. Your standing with Rome and your standing with the local peoples around you are tracked separately, and they don't always move together — currying favor with a provincial governor can cost you goodwill with your Gallic or Iberian neighbors, and vice versa. It's entirely possible to end up more trusted by the people next door than by the empire you nominally answer to.

### Your starting household

Whatever region you settle in, you begin with a small *familia*: your pater- or materfamilias (the character you'll be playing), a handful of family members, and a small staff of slaves and clients to run the place. It's modest by design — the game is about growing this into something larger, not starting there.

## A tour of the core screens

Once your household exists, you'll be moving between a small set of screens that between them cover everything you do:

- **Household Roster** — the full list of everyone in your familia: family, slaves, freedmen, clients, and any companions you've taken on. Each person here has their own record you can open for a closer look. This is where you assign labor, arrange marriages, and generally manage the people who make up your household.
- **Estate & Settlement** — your land and what's built on it: fields, workshops, warehouses, civic buildings, and everything else your holding produces or houses. This is where you spend resources to expand, upgrade, or diversify what your estate can do, and where a single villa can eventually grow into a village and beyond.
- **Monthly Report** — the summary that lands at the end of every month: what happened, what changed, and what needs your attention. This is also where random and scripted events surface for you to respond to.
- **Character Detail** — the individual record behind any single person in your world: their stats, traits, health, loyalty, relationships, and history. Every meaningful decision about a specific person — approving their marriage, assigning them a role, disciplining them — happens from their own detail screen.

## The rhythm of play

Time in *Gens* doesn't run continuously — you decide when it moves. At any point you can pause to review your household, plan, or just think, and advance time forward when you're ready to see what the next stretch brings.

The basic unit of time is the **month**. Each month, the game works through everything that needs to happen in a fixed, predictable order, so you always know what's about to be resolved before you advance:

1. Any orders and commitments you've already scheduled go first.
2. Life events — births, deaths, aging, comings of age — are resolved next.
3. Production runs: your fields, workshops, and other buildings generate their yield.
4. Labor and employment needs are settled — who's working where, and what still needs staffing.
5. Markets move and debts come due.
6. Relationships and the people around you (your household and beyond) act on their own motivations.
7. Hazards and disasters — fire, flood, drought, disease, and the like — have their chance to strike.
8. Random and scripted events fire, weighted by the people and circumstances actually involved.
9. Your Monthly Report is assembled from everything that just happened.
10. Finally, the game double-checks its own bookkeeping to make sure nothing has quietly gone inconsistent.

You don't see this checklist directly in play — it just means that when your Monthly Report lands, you can trust that everything behind it happened in a sensible, consistent sequence, not a scramble.

## Not everyone gets a full character sheet

The Roman world your household sits in is full of people — but the game doesn't track every single one of them in full detail, because most of them will never matter to your particular story. Most of the population around you is simulated only in the background, as anonymous groups: laborers, tenants, townsfolk, tracked in aggregate rather than as individuals.

The moment you actually interact with someone from that background — you hire them, someone proposes their marriage to you, you buy them at a slave market, or an event singles them out by name — the game promotes them into a full, named character with their own detail record, personality, and history from that point forward. Once someone has been promoted this way, they stay a full character for good; the game never quietly demotes a named person back into the anonymous crowd. This is why the cast of people you actually know grows naturally out of what you've actually done, rather than existing all at once from the start.

## What you know isn't always what's true

Not everything the game tells you about the world is guaranteed to be accurate. Rumors, dossiers, letters, and reports all represent what your household currently *believes*, not necessarily the unvarnished truth. A rival's finances, a slave's real temperament, a scheme brewing somewhere you can't see directly — these can be hidden from you entirely, known only approximately, or actively wrong, because someone lied to you or your information is out of date.

This is deliberate. The game keeps a clear line between what's actually happening in the world and what you currently know about it, and closing that gap — through espionage, travel, better relationships, or just time — is part of the game, not a bug in it. Don't take a report at face value just because it's the only information you have.

## Confirming a decision

Anything genuinely consequential — approving a marriage, issuing an edict, freeing a slave, sending soldiers into a fight — asks you to confirm it with the game's signature **wax seal**: press it down, and the decision is set. It's the one place the interface allows itself real ceremony, precisely so it's obvious which choices in this game are the ones that actually matter.

## Saving and loading

Your progress is saved to your own campaign file, which you can return to and load at any time. Saves carry forward cleanly across game updates — if a save was made under an older version of the game, it's brought up to date automatically when you load it, so you never need to worry about a save becoming unreadable.
