# Welcome to Gens

*Gens* is a Roman household-management game: you take charge of a *familia* — a family, its slaves, freedmen, and clients — and grow it, over one lifetime or across ten generations, into an estate, a settlement, or a genuine political dynasty. There's no map to conquer and no fixed win condition. You set your own goals, and the game keeps a permanent, readable record of everything your household does on the way to reaching (or missing) them.

You'll start small: a modest plot of land, a handful of relatives, a few slaves and clients under your roof. From there, everything is your choice. Build a trading empire. Raise soldiers and chase military glory. Climb the rungs of Roman office. Found temples in your family's name. Or simply hold the land, generation after generation, and let the estate endure. Every path is equally real; the game doesn't quietly steer you toward one "correct" way to play.

## Where it comes from

If you've played *Free Cities*, the shape of *Gens* will feel immediately familiar: a hub screen you return to again and again, deep personal records for every single person in your world, random events that trigger off those people's own stats and traits, and slow, grinding growth from a tiny starting holding into something much bigger. *Gens* borrows that skeleton wholesale and rebuilds it around Roman household life instead.

Two other games shaped what happens on top of that skeleton. *Crusader Kings* supplies the dynastic engine — succession is your choice, contested inheritances create real drama, and adoption is a genuine political tool rather than an afterthought, the same way Augustus used it to build the Julio-Claudian succession. *Total War* supplies the economic backbone — building chains across farming, industry, trade, civic life, and the military, with no single chain treated as the "right" one to climb. What *Gens* deliberately leaves behind from both of those games is their map: there's no strategic map to maneuver armies across and no tactical battles to fight out tile by tile. Your world is your household, your estate, and the people connected to it — not a battlefield.

## Why these nine ideas run the whole game

*Gens* is built around nine commitments. They're not just marketing copy — each one directly shapes a decision you'll make while playing.

1. **Every person is real, not a resource.** The cook, the slave working your fields, your least-favorite nephew — each one is a full character with stats, a personality, loyalty toward you, and their own trajectory through life. You'll come to recognize people, not line items.
2. **There's no finish line.** No victory screen waits for you. A playthrough can wrap up in a single lifetime or run ten generations deep, and it succeeds or fails purely by whatever standard you set for it.
3. **Any economic identity is a real identity.** Farming, trade, manufacturing, and soldiering are all equally legitimate ways to build a household's fortune — the game never nudges you toward one as the "true" path to power.
4. **Harsh subjects are handled honestly.** Slavery and political violence carry real weight and real consequences in play — see the tone section below for exactly what that means and doesn't mean.
5. **You play one person; you govern everyone else.** You directly control your pater- or materfamilias, but everyone else in your household acts on their own loyalty, personality, and interests — issuing an order doesn't guarantee it gets carried out the way you intended.
6. **The world doesn't wait for you.** Rival households are chasing the same land, offices, marriages, and prestige you are, on their own schedule, whether or not you're paying attention to them.
7. **Your history matters and you can read it.** Every birth, death, marriage, scandal, and triumph accumulates into your dynasty's own Chronicle — a game about lineage that actually lets you sit down and read your lineage.
8. **Governing is policy, not a checklist.** Laws, standing household policy, and one-off edicts are lasting decisions with real tradeoffs attached, not disposable pop-up prompts you click through once.
9. **The world outside your gate is worth reaching.** Travel, letters, court appointments, and personal relationships pull play outward into a wider social and geographic world — the game isn't only about staring at your own ledger.

## Setting

By default, *Gens* is set during the **Pax Romana** — the long stretch of Roman stability that gives a household-building game a firm floor to build on. But the setting isn't locked to one decade: in practice the game's historical range stretches from the reforms of the Gracchi (133 BC) through Justinian's reconquests (to roughly AD 565), so you can push earlier into the turbulence of the late Republic's frontier colonization, or later into the early Dominate, without anything in the core game breaking underneath you.

## A note on tone and content

*Gens* treats slavery, political brutality, and violence with real mechanical stakes and an unflinching narrative voice. Punishing a slave, standing trial for your life, or sending household soldiers into a fight are all played straight, with consequences that matter — not softened for comfort, and not staged for shock value either. This is a serious game about a genuinely harsh historical world, and it doesn't look away from that.

At the same time, *Gens* is not a game about sex. Romance and seduction are fully built out as systems — they carry real political and relational weight, feeding marriages, alliances, leverage, and blackmail — but the sexual dimension of any relationship is always kept off-screen: implied, faded to black, described the way a serious historical drama handles it, never depicted and never resolved as its own mechanic. If you're wondering what kind of game this is going to be about that subject: this is the answer.

## What it looks like

*Gens* is built to feel like you're paging through an actual Roman household's own paperwork, not a conventional strategy-game skin. Most screens open as a **wax-tablet diptych** — two hinged leaves, your identity and key numbers on the left, the actual business of the screen on the right — under a persistent **ink-bar** spine that always shows your household's name, the date, your treasury, and your standing. A restrained seven-color palette (papyrus, iron-gall ink, Tyrian purple, terracotta, verdigris bronze, gold leaf, and a crisis-only oxide red) carries consistent meaning everywhere, so you'll learn to read the game's colors the way you'd learn any other system. Every decision that actually matters — approving a marriage, issuing an edict, freeing a slave — is confirmed with a **wax seal** that presses down when you commit to it. And your dynasty's own history lives in the **Chronicle**, presented as a single illuminated scroll that unfurls further the longer your household survives.

## What you'll actually do

- **Run a household** — assign labor, arrange marriages, raise children, manage slaves and freedmen, and watch loyalty and ambition play out among people you actually know by name.
- **Grow an estate** — build up production across farming, industry, trade, civic works, and the military, and expand your physical landholding from a single villa toward a village and eventually a town.
- **Play politics** — cultivate patrons and clients, stand for local office, and work toward the distant heights of a Roman political career.
- **Practice governance** — set standing policy on taxation, labor, and religious rites, issue edicts, and fund games and public works as lasting, revisable choices.
- **Travel and correspond** — send people to Rome, a rival's estate, or the frontier, or manage those relationships by letter when a trip isn't worth the risk.
- **Build a dynasty** — choose your own succession, use adoption as a real political tool, and watch your family's history accumulate into something worth reading back.
