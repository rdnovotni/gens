# Culture, Religion & Knowledge

A household is more than its ledgers and its swords. This chapter covers the life of the mind and the soul: the gods you keep, the education you give your children, the books and art you collect, the discoveries your age is making, and the monuments you leave behind. None of it is idle flavor — piety moves real outcomes, learning opens real doors, and a lost book or a toppled statue is a real, permanent loss.

## Religion

Every Roman household keeps its gods, and yours is no exception. At the center of your religious life sits **Favor**, a single tracked value representing your standing with the divine — a real, distinct axis from Dignitas (what Rome and your peers think of you). The two usually move together, but they can diverge sharply, and that divergence is where things get interesting: a household Rome respects highly but the gods apparently do not is a real and unsettling story of its own.

When you found your household (or at a new head's succession), you choose a **Patron Deity** — Jupiter, Juno, Mars, Venus, Minerva, Ceres, Neptune, Mercury, Vesta, Apollo, Diana, or Bacchus — and your choice shapes what Favor actually does for you. High Favor under Mars sharpens your recruiting and military morale; under Ceres it stabilizes your harvests; under Venus it smooths your marriages and romances; under Neptune it guards you against the sea and against pirates specifically. Low Favor doesn't just withhold the bonus — it exposes you to an **Ill Omen** themed to your own patron's domain: a Mars household risks a military-disaster omen, a Ceres household a blight portent. Choosing a patron is choosing which part of your life you're betting your religious devotion on, for better and for worse. You can change patrons later — most naturally at a succession, or after some major event plausibly read as divine intervention — but reconsecrating resets your accumulated Favor toward neutral, and a household that does it often reads as fickle to traditionalist eyes.

Favor accrues from sustained daily worship, funded festivals, heeded omens, holding a state priesthood, and a well-tended shrine; it erodes from neglect, impiety, broken oaths, and scandal touching a priest or Vestal. Let it fall far enough and your household enters **Divine Displeasure** — never a hard failure state, but a real, standing vulnerability: omens turn more frequent and harsher, traditionalist opinion sours independent of your actual Dignitas, and your people's own contentment quietly suffers from serving patrons the gods seem to have abandoned.

Day to day, your **Lares** (household guardians) and **Penates** (pantry gods) are honored at your home shrine, the Lararium, tended by your household priest (the Sacerdos Domesticus) or, absent one, by you personally. A household head's own guardian spirit — the **Genius**, or **Juno** for a materfamilias — is honored alongside them, flavor rather than a separately tracked number, but real and worth knowing about. You set a standing **Rites Budget** — Frugal, Standard, or Lavish — trading ongoing expense against Favor stability, exactly the kind of durable policy choice this game favors over one-off decisions.

Beyond daily worship, the gods speak in two ways. **Omens** surface as passive events — a flight of birds, a strange dream — each posing a real choice: heed it at some real, modest cost, or ignore it and risk the consequence landing anyway. **Auspices** are the active version: commissioning a formal reading before a major decision (a military campaign, a long journey, a marriage) for an informed read on how it's likely to go, performed reliably by your household priest or, far better, by a Character holding the **Augur** office. A handful of real named festivals — Saturnalia, Lupercalia, the Vestalia, Ceres's own Cerealia, and others — sit on your sacred calendar, observed passively for a small automatic tick or funded properly for a real, larger payoff in Favor, standing, and public contentment.

Beyond the household shrine, a state **Priesthood** track runs alongside ordinary political office — Augur and Flamen (a priest dedicated to your own patron deity) as achievable rungs, with a capstone **Pontifex** role as a rare, prestigious endpoint. And in a genuinely rare, high-stakes case, a young unmarried woman of your family can be dedicated as a **Vestal Virgin** — a real, historically frank institution: she's exempt from your household's ordinary legal authority over her for the length of her service, held to an extremely severe chastity requirement, and, on completing her tenure honorably, returns to civilian life with exceptional standing and her own independent property rights.

Your household's beliefs don't have to stay purely Roman. **Foreign cults** — Isis, Cybele, Mithras, Judaism, and, in a late enough setting, early Christianity — are real, period-accurate presences an individual household member can quietly adopt on a personal level at little risk, or that your whole household can formally embrace as a genuine practice alongside (or in place of) your Roman patron. That heavier commitment brings a distinctive benefit no ordinary Roman deity offers, weighed against real backlash from a traditionalist audience and, taken far enough, a genuine risk of persecution.

Piety is a real personality trait spectrum, running Impious to Indifferent to Devout to Zealous — see the [Household & Family chapter](03-household-and-family.md) for how traits generally work. An Impious character forfeits Favor's benefits outright but shrugs off an ignored omen's penalty; a Zealous one gets the sharpest version of both the reward and the cost.

For Favor's full mechanics, the complete feast calendar, and the priesthood ladder, see [Religion](../design/gens-religion-design.md). Your household's relationship with the Roman dead — ancestor veneration, funerals, and mourning — is a related but genuinely separate subject covered in the Household & Family chapter.

## Education & Culture

How you raise and educate your household's minds shapes what they can become. Childhood education follows a real Roman age-based curriculum, delivered by a household tutor (Paedagogus) or a settlement's Rhetor or teacher, culminating in adolescence with a genuine choice of **Educational Track**: Rhetoric (sharpening diplomacy), Philosophy (learning and cultural standing), or the Gymnasium (martial skill and learning together). A gifted household can go further and hire a genuinely **Distinguished** tutor for a real quality upgrade over the ordinary local path.

The single richest option, though, is sending a promising son or daughter abroad to study at a real **Institution of Renown** — the Academy at Athens (the single best destination for Philosophy), Rhodes (the single best destination for Rhetoric, the very school Cicero sent his own son to), Alexandria (general learning, with a distinct medical specialty drawing on its real, legendary Library), Pergamon (a real, close second to Alexandria, also strong in medicine), or Massilia (the closer, safer Western option, a genuine "Athens of Gaul" for a household that can't spare years for the voyage east). Sending someone away is a real multi-year journey, carrying every risk travel already carries, and a real ongoing cost for tuition and living expenses — plus a real, felt gamble: a Character who studies at a Hellenic institution comes home genuinely more Greek than when they left, the sharpest single lever in the whole game on cultural drift. What you get back is a permanent **Institution Credential** — faster progress in their chosen Track for life, a real edge in the marriage market, and, for Alexandria or Pergamon specifically, a genuine medical bonus if they go on to serve as your household physician. Build your own household's academy or library prestigious enough, and the flow can even reverse — foreign students seeking you out in turn.

All of this feeds a real, tracked **Culture** identity for every character (Roman, Gallic, Iberian, Hellenic, or a blend), and literacy and career access both gate on it: illiteracy blocks record-keeping roles and correspondence outright, contesting anything above the lowest magistracy requires the Rhetoric Track, and the best marriage candidates aren't offered below a real cultural-prestige threshold.

Beyond childhood, education becomes **Cultural Patronage** — an adult, household-level pursuit built around hosting a **Symposium** and sponsoring **Literary Patronage** for a poet or scholar, both real, ongoing sources of cultural prestige. Enough sustained investment here pulls your household toward Hellenization, a real tension against Roman traditionalist sentiment that the game plays honestly in both directions — one path is never simply correct.

For the full Pedagogy pipeline, the Institution roster, and the Hellenization tension in detail, see [Education & Culture](../design/gens-education-culture-design.md).

## Language & Literacy

Two related but genuinely distinct stats sit downstream of everything above, not a competing system of their own: **Literacy** (can this person actually read and write) and **Language Proficiency** (which spoken languages they know, and how well). Both are derived, by default, straight from the Learning investment your Education & Culture choices already produce — you don't grind them separately.

For most of your world, literacy isn't even tracked as a number — it's simply assumed from legal status and wealth, exactly as real history would suggest. For a named character it matters, it becomes a real, visible fact — with one deliberately honest historical wrinkle worth knowing: an educated Greek slave or freedman scribe was often genuinely more literate than the Roman citizen who owned them, and your household's own literacy, wherever it matters, is often really the literacy of whoever holds your Tabularius (record-keeper) or Tutor position.

Language works on a simple four-tier scale — None, Basic, Conversational, and Fluent/Native — and every region in the game has a real historical dominant tongue your characters acquire natively by birth, and can build further through formal education (especially at an Institution of Renown), sustained exposure while traveling or holding land abroad, or a traveling teacher. Related languages come easier than unrelated ones — a Gaulish speaker picks up Brythonic faster than an entirely unrelated tongue. Most everyday interaction across a language gap is only softly penalized, awkward but manageable — but formal diplomacy with a foreign people is a real hard gate: you genuinely cannot negotiate without either a fluent negotiator or a qualified **Interpres**, a household position built specifically to solve that problem on a standing basis. There's no cap on how many languages a sufficiently learned character can hold — real elite Romans routinely managed genuine trilingualism as an unremarkable baseline.

For the complete language map and its regional groupings, see [Language & Literacy](../design/gens-language-literacy-design.md).

## Books & Manuscripts

Owning a well-stocked library (your Bibliotheca) speeds education passively, the way it always has — but this game also lets your library hold real, individually-tracked treasures rather than an undifferentiated stack of scrolls. A **Work** is the actual intellectual content — a poem, a history, a philosophical treatise, a map — authored once by a specific character; a **Copy** is a physical object embodying it, hand-copied onto tablets, parchment, or papyrus, each one carrying its own condition and its own independent history. Because nothing here is printed, a Work survives exactly as long as at least one Copy of it does anywhere in the world — and losing the very last Copy is a genuine, permanent loss.

Ten existing traits — Historian, Poet, Playwright, Philosopher, Theologian, Legal Scholar, Genealogist, Astrologer, Naturalist, and Cartographer — finally get a real payoff here: a character holding one can undertake **Authorship**, producing a genuine new Work in their specialty. Anyone sufficiently literate can attempt something modest even without the matching trait, but the specialist produces real quality and a real shot at lasting significance. The very first copy ever made, in the author's own hand, is flagged forever as the **Autograph** — a real, permanent head start toward becoming a formally **Renowned Copy**, the more history accumulates on it. A bilingual character can also produce a **Translated Copy** of a foreign work, quality depending directly on their own fluency.

You can acquire books by commissioning them directly, buying them, receiving them as gifts, inheriting them, capturing them as war spoils, stumbling on a forgotten manuscript while traveling, or stealing a rival's irreplaceable copy outright. And you can lose them: to fire, to ordinary neglect and decay, or to deliberate destruction. When a Work's last copy is destroyed, the game checks honestly whether any other surviving work quotes or cites it — if so, it survives diminished, as a genuine **Fragmentary** work, rather than vanishing outright; if not, it's formally and permanently **Lost**, with no safety net, mirroring the sobering real fact that most ancient literature genuinely didn't survive. A copy's stated authorship isn't always to be trusted, either — a **Forgery** falsely attributed to a famous name is a real, if rare, possibility, undetected until it isn't. And reading a book isn't just for show: spending real time with a specific work nudges a character's own learning and can help toward acquiring a matching trait.

A well-tended household library, under a dedicated Bibliothecarius, is real prestige in its own right — and donating a duplicate or a genuinely valuable copy to your settlement's public library is a real act of civic generosity, and makes that copy meaningfully safer than it would be on your own private shelf.

For content categories, provenance mechanics, and the forgery and translation rules in full, see [Books & Manuscripts](../design/gens-books-manuscripts-design.md).

## Art & Art Commissions, and Masterworks

Your household's art collection can be just as individually real as its library. **Art & Art Commissions** gives visual art the same treatment books get, built on a genuine, well-documented piece of Roman history: an enormous share of ancient sculpture that survives today does so only as a later marble copy of a lost Greek bronze — the bronze itself long since melted down. A commissioned **Original** and any later **Replica** made by a different hand both track their own quality and history independently, and a Work — the underlying artistic idea — survives as long as any Original or Replica of it does anywhere. Real named artisan positions carry the actual craft: a Sculptor works stone and bronze, a Pictor paints panels and frescoes, a Musivarius makes mosaics, a Caelator engraves metal reliefs, and a Gemmarius cuts gems and cameo glass. A renowned artist's apprentice inherits a real head start of their own; when an artist dies, every surviving work of theirs gains a permanent boost simply because there will never be another. You choose not just a medium but a subject — a family portrait, a mythological scene, or, if you deliberately choose it, a specific moment from your own Dynasty Chronicle rendered as art. Bronze and silver pieces face a real risk of being melted down for their metal in hard times; marble can instead be re-carved into a new subject rather than destroyed outright — the actual mechanism behind Damnatio Memoriae's own "statues smashed or re-carved."

**Masterworks & Unique Crafted Objects** covers everything else worth making singular and unrepeatable: weapons and armor equipped by a specific person rather than folded into an abstract squad's gear, a military standard that anchors a squad's morale while intact, a signet ring that genuinely authenticates a sealed letter, a poison ring (a real, dark tool for a coercive scheme, deeply incriminating if ever discovered), the regalia of a magistrate's office, a physician's instrument kit, even a real geared astronomical mechanism as a vanishingly rare marvel-tier find. Unlike a book or a painting, a Masterwork was never meant to be reproduced — there's no copy to fall back on, so its loss is always total. But a damaged one can genuinely be repaired, and a captured one can be recovered — through ransom, through the open market, or through a war fought specifically to win it back. Designate one as an **Heirloom** and it routes automatically to your next head of household regardless of how the rest of the estate divides; a sufficiently storied object rises to Renowned and, rarest of all, a true **Legendary** tier where the object's own name carries weight independent of whoever currently owns it. And an object doesn't have to be beloved to matter — one tied to a real betrayal or disaster can carry a standing **Ill Repute**, feared by the superstitious and, for a bold enough household, worn as its own strange kind of prestige.

For the full medium and category tables, provenance rules, and functional bonuses, see [Art & Art Commissions](../design/gens-art-art-commissions-design.md) and [Masterworks & Unique Crafted Objects](../design/gens-masterworks-unique-crafted-objects-design.md).

## Technology & Discoveries

History doesn't stand still around you, and neither does technology. Every real innovation and reform of the Roman age — from currency standardization to the Pantheon's dome to Justinian's legal code — is a named **Discovery** with its own real historical arrival year. Play entirely hands-off and every Discovery simply appears in the world exactly on its real historical schedule; you never have to touch this system at all if you'd rather not.

But you can also push ahead of that schedule. Fund an Institution of Renown or your own household's research effort (**Institutional Patronage**), or have a sufficiently learned character write a genuine technical treatise on the subject themselves (**Individual Genius**) — either is a real gamble, not a guaranteed purchase; a failed attempt doesn't refund your investment, but it leaves a real, permanent head start toward trying again. Succeed, and the Discovery becomes your household's own **exclusive early edge** for as long as you can keep it — a real, if temporary, advantage no one else has access to yet, and a real espionage target a rival can try to steal, gaining a parallel edge of their own without erasing yours. Two households can even race for the same breakthrough at once; whoever finishes first claims it, and the loser's investment isn't wasted either.

Eventually you face a real choice: keep your edge exclusive as long as you can defend it, or **publish** it — through a written treatise or a civic gift — trading your exclusivity for outsized prestige and standing. A published Discovery doesn't spread instantly everywhere; it moves through the same real trade-route and cultural-contact channels that spread culture generally, reaching a well-connected city long before it reaches an isolated frontier outpost. And in the single most thematically pointed twist in this whole system, a Discovery that's still narrow or freshly published can genuinely be **lost again** if its only detailed record is destroyed and no one else has yet independently learned it — a direct, honest echo of the real, well-documented fact that real Roman engineering knowledge actually did fade for generations after the Western Empire's decline before being rediscovered.

The **Discovery Roster** is the actual content behind this engine — the real, individually named list of every Discovery in the game, organized into Eras that are pure organizational buckets (nothing unlocks because an Era arrives; only because a specific Discovery's own year passes, or you accelerate it), covering everything from Marius's military reforms and the real Acta Diurna public gazette in the Republican era through to Justinian-era dome engineering and the *Corpus Juris Civilis*. Where the engine defines how discoveries arrive, race, spread, and vanish, the Roster is simply the list of what's actually out there to find.

For the complete acceleration, diffusion, and lost-knowledge mechanics, see [Technology & Discoveries](../design/gens-technology-discoveries-design.md); for the full list of individual discoveries by era, see the [Discovery Roster](../design/gens-discovery-roster-design.md).

## Monuments & Legacy Building

Some things you build aren't meant to produce anything at all — they're meant to be remembered. A **Monument** — a statue, a family tomb, a dedicatory temple, a triumphal arch earned by a genuine military victory, and several rarer types tied to specific achievements elsewhere in the game — produces no yield whatsoever. What it produces is **Dignitas** and a permanent entry in your Dynasty Chronicle, and, done well enough, something rival houses genuinely notice and react to.

A Monument's real value is its **Legacy**: the longer it survives real generational successions intact, the more it's worth, climbing from New through Established to a rare Ancient tier. You can even turn an ordinary civic improvement into one — funding a Public Works project at a large enough scale lets you attach your family's name to it permanently, exactly the real Roman practice you can still read on countless surviving inscriptions, rather than letting the spend simply fade the month after you make it. How many Monuments you can meaningfully support scales with how large your settlement actually is — a handful for a modest villa, a real substantial collection for a full city — so building extensively here gives you a real reason to also invest in growing your estate.

Monuments aren't invulnerable. Natural disasters can damage them, and sufficiently long neglect after damage can cost a full Legacy Tier permanently. And there's a darker fate reserved for real disgrace: **Damnatio Memoriae**, the actual, historically documented Roman practice of formally condemning a name and ordering every trace of it erased — statues smashed or re-carved, inscriptions chiseled out, a name struck from public record. A household that suffers a severe enough catastrophe — a failed rebellion against Rome, a ruinous political purge, the worst possible outcome of a capital legal case — can face this directly, every Monument it holds defaced and its own name marked, in-fiction, as officially erased (though nothing is ever truly deleted from your actual Chronicle — the game's own commitment to preserving what really happened holds even here). The reverse is equally real: a sufficiently powerful household can petition to have this fate formally imposed on an already-defeated rival, the single most severe political victory available short of destroying them outright. And, rarely, history can even be reversed — a new regime, a changed political climate, and a defaced Monument can be restored, its legacy beginning to rebuild from nothing.

For the full Monument roster, the Legacy Value mechanics, and Damnatio Memoriae's complete rules, see [Monuments & Legacy Building](../design/gens-monuments-legacy-building-design.md).
