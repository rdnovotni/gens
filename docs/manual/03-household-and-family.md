# Household and Family

Everything in *Gens* eventually comes back to your *familia* — not just your bloodline, but every person your household actually holds: family by blood and marriage, the people you own, the people you freed, the clients who depend on your patronage, and the staff and companions who keep the whole thing running. This chapter is about that household — how it's built, who's in it, what drives each of them as individuals, and how it changes shape across a lifetime and across generations. Full mechanical depth for every system named here lives in the linked design documents; this chapter tells you what you need to actually play.

## What a Familia Is

In Roman law and in *Gens*, a *familia* was never just "your relatives." It's the whole household under your authority: your spouse and children, any slaves you own, any freedmen who remain bound to you by ongoing obligation even after manumission, the clients attached to your name, and the companions who serve in a household position. Every one of these people is tracked with the same depth — the same five core abilities (Diplomacy, Martial, Stewardship, Intrigue, Learning), the same health, loyalty, and ambition, the same web of personal relationships to everyone else in your world. A field hand and a senator's daughter are built from the identical schema; what differs is their legal standing and their station, not the game's willingness to take them seriously. Full detail on this stat architecture lives in [Familia](../design/gens-familia-design.md).

**Legal status** is tracked separately from wealth or class, and it has real teeth. A Roman Citizen can own property, marry other citizens, and testify in court. Latin Rights holders and Peregrines (free non-citizens) carry fewer privileges. A Freedman is legally free but remains bound by *obsequium* — an ongoing duty of respect and service to the patron who freed them — and can't hold the highest offices, though their own children are born fully free. The Enslaved have no independent legal personhood at all; manumission is their only path out. None of these categories are permanent traps: status moves, through manumission, citizenship grants, or simple birth into freedom.

### Lifecycle

Every person in your household passes through five life stages — Infant, Child, Adolescent, Adult, and Elderly — and each stage opens or closes what that person can actually do. An Infant is pure survival stakes: no duties, real risk of not making it to Childhood. Formal schooling and personality begin taking shape in Childhood. Adolescence opens betrothal and marriage negotiations and the first labor assignments. Adulthood is where a person can do everything — hold office, marry, fight, scheme, run a business. The Elderly stage brings gradual decline in ability and health, but also opens *retirement* as a real, deliberate choice rather than a forced ending — more on what that actually looks like in [Growing Old](#growing-old), below. Death itself is never softened or prevented by the game; old age, disease, violence, and disaster can all end a life, and every death has somewhere real to register — in the household's own memory, in succession, in the web of people who knew them. See [Familia](../design/gens-familia-design.md) for the full stage table.

### Headship and Marriage

One person holds headship of the household — historically the *paterfamilias*, and by default a male role, matching the setting rather than softening it (a start-of-game toggle can relax this for players who'd rather not play within it). The head sets policy, approves marriages, chooses an heir, and answers for the household's conduct.

Marriage in *Gens* runs on real Roman logic rather than a modern one: for most of the game's timeframe, a marriage needed no ceremony and no state registration to be legally real. What actually made two people married was ***affectio maritalis*** — the genuine, ongoing, mutual intent of both parties to be married, shown through living together and being publicly treated as spouses. A formal betrothal often preceded it, sometimes marked by exchanging an iron ring, but the wedding celebration itself — however grand — was a social and religious occasion layered on top of a legal fact that didn't strictly require it. (See [Romance, Marriage, and Lineage](#romance-marriage-and-lineage) below for how courtship, weddings, and family-founding actually play out.)

**Legitimacy** matters directly to who can inherit. A child born inside a recognized marriage is legitimate automatically. A child born of an affair is not — unless the head of household deliberately and visibly acknowledges them, a choice that carries its own social cost. Illegitimacy doesn't permanently bar someone from the succession, but it does mean they need that explicit intervention, or formal adoption, where a legitimate heir needs neither.

## Every Person Is a Character

The single most important thing to understand about *Gens*: there is no "resource tier" for people. The cook, a field slave, a rival senator's rival cousin, and your own firstborn are all built from the exact same underlying record — a **Character**. Anyone the game names specifically — whether they were born in your house, hired into it, met on the road, or encountered as a political rival — gets the complete schema the instant they're named: full abilities, a personality, a place in the relationship web, and the ability to act on their own initiative rather than simply sitting still until you click on them. Full detail lives in [Characters](../design/gens-characters-design.md).

### Personality Axes

Underneath the visible traits (below) sits a hidden layer of seven **Personality Axes** — Honor, Compassion, Greed, Zealotry, Vengefulness, Boldness, and Rationality — running from one extreme to the other. You never see these numbers directly, but they're what actually decides whether a character keeps a promise, accepts a bribe, forgives a slight, or starts a scheme of their own. Traits nudge these axes; the axes, in turn, drive both the dice-roll resolution behind formal contests (an election, a scheme) and the tone of any open-ended conversation the game narrates for you. A Treacherous, Greedy character genuinely won't fold to a heartfelt appeal the way a Loyal, Compassionate one might — the axes are what makes that consistent rather than random.

### Traits and the Trait Catalog

Every character carries a handful of **traits** — tags describing innate temperament, upbringing, and lived experience — drawn from a catalog of well over two hundred, authoritative in [Traits](../design/gens-traits-design.md). A typical adult holds perhaps six to ten total; nobody holds anywhere near the whole pool, which is exactly what keeps a character reading as a specific person rather than a checklist.

Traits fall into three families:

- **Congenital** traits are rolled at birth, weighted by what the parents themselves carry — things like *Honest/Deceitful*, *Bold/Cautious*, or *Generous/Greedy*.
- **Formative** traits form during Childhood and Adolescence, shaped by upbringing and education — *Studious/Incurious*, *Trusting/Cynical*, *Frugal/Extravagant*.
- **Reactive** traits are earned in adulthood, from how a person is actually treated and what happens to them — *Content/Resentful*, *Traumatized/Resilient*, *Corrupt/Incorruptible*. These are the only traits that can flip later in life: years of good or ill treatment genuinely change who someone is.

Most traits come in opposed pairs — you can't be both Brave and Craven — but five traits are instead **tiered spectrums**, a four-rung scale everyone holds exactly one position on rather than a simple on/off switch:

- **Intellect** (Dull → Average → Clever → Brilliant)
- **Beauty** (Hideous → Plain → Comely → Beautiful)
- **Physique** (Frail → Average → Strong → Herculean)
- **Piety** (Impious → Indifferent → Devout → Zealous)
- **The Four Humors** — Sanguine, Choleric, Melancholic, or Phlegmatic, the setting's own period-appropriate personality framework, borrowed straight from ancient medicine rather than a modern invention.

Adults can also pick up **Lifestyle traits** through sustained practice — *Physician*, *Poet*, *Merchant's Instinct*, *Spymaster* — real specializations rather than temperament, capped at three held at once so the category stays a meaningful identity rather than an ever-growing badge collection.

Whenever a character's held traits line up with a recognized pairing, the game generates a **Combo Title** — a short, headline description shown next to their name, in the same vein as "Treacherous Hero" or "Devoted Caregiver." A Deceitful, Zealous character reads as a *Pious Fraud*; a Ruthless, Disciplined one as an *Iron Hand*. These titles aren't fixed for life — because Reactive traits can shift, a Combo Title recalculates whenever the underlying traits meaningfully change, so a compassionate priest ground down by a hard decade can genuinely stop reading as a "Devoted Caregiver" and start reading as something darker.

## Ambitions

Beyond the household's own institutional identity and the rare, distant peaks of a political or military career, every character can carry a **Primary Ambition** — a specific, personal, checkable goal scaled to one lifetime rather than a whole dynasty. "Marry into House Fabricia," "Build a Fortune," "Earn Manumission," and "Settle the Score" are all Ambitions; each is broken into two to four concrete milestones that resolve through systems you already use elsewhere — an election won, a threshold of wealth crossed, a scheme completed. Full detail is in [Character Ambitions](../design/gens-character-ambitions-design.md).

For your own character, choosing an Ambition (or leaving the slot empty) is a real, deliberate decision. Every other character in your world generates one automatically from their own personality and station — a high-Greed companion quietly chases wealth, a Vengeful client nurses a grudge — which is exactly how a Procurator who's "acting in their own interest" or a rival house's head who's "up to something" becomes a legible, specific story rather than an unexplained risk. An Ambition can be **completed** (a real, celebrated milestone, sometimes earning a lasting epithet), **abandoned** with no penalty, or **thwarted** — which is the consequential outcome, routing directly into a Reactive trait like Resentful or Vengeful. An Ambition left unfinished at death can even become a **Legacy Ambition**, offered — never forced — to the heir who comes after.

## Companions & Court Positions

As your household grows, you'll want people running it who aren't you. *Gens* organizes appointable staff into three tiers, detailed in full in [Companions & Court Positions](../design/gens-companions-court-positions-design.md):

- **Labor duty slots** — Field Hand, Cook, Craftsman, and the like — are open to any eligible household member and drawn from their Labor Skills rather than the five core abilities.
- **Overseers** sit above a single building or a tight cluster of them — a *Vilicus* running your fields, an *Institor* running a trading post, a *Venalicius* managing a slave market — reading mostly Stewardship, and raising or dragging down that operation's output depending on how good they are.
- **Senior/Court Positions** run a whole villa, an entire settlement-wide category, or eventually a second settlement outright — a Steward (*Dispensator*), a Household Spymaster, a Court Physician, a Praefectus Vigilum commanding the watch. This is the largest and richest tier: by the time your estate reaches city scale you could plausibly be staffing two dozen distinct named positions, though nothing forces you to fill them all — an empty post just means that room or duty runs at its unstaffed default.

These tiers form a real promotion ladder. A standout in a duty slot can rise to Overseer; a proven Overseer can rise to a Senior Position; and status is never a hard ceiling — an enslaved individual, a freedman, a client, or a family member can all occupy any tier, exactly the way a talented household-born son might become Steward without ever running a single field first.

The **Travel Retinue** — who physically accompanies you on a journey — isn't a separate character type at all; it's simply which existing household members you bring along, each contributing whatever they already are: a Bodyguard reduces ambush risk, a Secretary keeps your correspondence flowing on the road, a Court Physician reduces disease exposure. Travel is also the household's main gateway for recruiting genuinely new people — a promising client's son, a stranded freedman, a skilled captive can join the household outright and immediately take a full place in it.

One boundary worth being explicit about: none of this creates an actual system of Roman **political office**. A Procurator or a Praefectus is a private household appointment managing your own domestic and economic interests — even where the title borrows a real Roman term. Standing for an actual magistracy, and the long climb of a political career, belongs to a different part of the game entirely.

## Growing Up

Childhood in *Gens* has its own real ritual scaffolding, layered on top of the education investment you make in a child's formal schooling (that pipeline — tutors, curriculum tracks, literacy — is its own subject; see [Coming of Age & Education](../design/gens-coming-of-age-education-design.md) for the full sequence). A newborn is formally named at the *dies lustricus*, a real household ceremony held a few days after birth, and receives a protective amulet — a *bulla* for a boy, a *lunula* for a girl — worn throughout childhood. Around age seven, formal schooling actually begins, working through three real historical stages: basic literacy and arithmetic with a *litterator*, deeper grammar and literature with a *grammaticus*, and eventually formal oratory with a *rhetor*.

A young man's real coming-of-age is the ***toga virilis*** — his own full ceremonial occasion, complete with dedicating his childhood *bulla*, first wearing the plain adult toga, a public procession to the Forum, and a closing feast. It's often, though not always, timed to the festival of Liberalia. Immediately afterward, many young men enter a real apprenticeship, the ***tirocinium*** — shadowing an established mentor in either public life or the army — which forms a genuine, lasting bond and grants a real head start into a political or military career later on.

Roman culture offered girls no equivalent public ceremony or apprenticeship; that's an honest asymmetry the game doesn't paper over. A young woman's path to adult standing instead runs through marriage, and her own small, real observance — dedicating her childhood toys and her *lunula* — happens as part of her wedding preparations rather than a separate rite of its own.

## Romance, Marriage, and Lineage

Most of your household's romantic and sexual life happens the way it did for real Roman families of any size: mostly in the background, among people you're not directly controlling, surfaced to you through the household's monthly reports and its Chronicle rather than requiring your constant attention. A widowed cousin quietly takes a lover; a son fathers a child at a distant posting; a married daughter's old friendship finally becomes something more. The game escalates to a real decision only when the stakes actually justify it — a rival house is involved, a child's legitimacy is contested, an important marriage is directly threatened. Full mechanics live in [Romance, Sexuality & Lineage](../design/gens-romance-sexuality-lineage-design.md) — note that this document, not the older *Romance & Seduction* material, is the authoritative source; treat any older wedding/romance flavor you may recall as superseded by what's described here and in the Weddings system below.

Two things are never negotiable, regardless of any other system: nothing here ever applies to a character below adulthood, and no relationship touching an enslaved person and their own owner is ever read through this romantic system at all — that dynamic is handled honestly, and separately, by the game's slavery mechanics.

Every romantic bond tracks two separate numbers — **Affection** (emotional warmth and trust) and **Attraction** (physical and romantic desire) — so a marriage built purely on political convenience reads as a genuinely different story than a passionate but shallow affair, even though both might show the same relationship "opinion" on paper. Courtship (flirting, wooing, confessing feelings) builds both over time and can lead to a genuine love-match marriage — Roman marriage's own affectio maritalis in action — sitting alongside the transactional **arranged marriage** model, which is calculated from dowry, alliance value, and family prestige, with real consent and compatibility affecting long-term happiness even though consent was never legally required.

Between marriage and a hidden affair sits a real, legally-recognized middle ground: the **Concubine** bond. Roman law barred certain pairings from marriage outright — a senator couldn't marry a freedwoman, for instance — and concubinage was the honest, legally acknowledged alternative: publicly known rather than secret, with the same Affection/Attraction tracking and real pregnancy risk as a marriage, but never producing a legitimacy claim and never subject to formal divorce. It simply ends when either party chooses.

Same-sex relationships exist as their own fully modeled, fully dignified track, entirely separate from the heir-producing marriage market that Roman law itself never extended to them — they carry the same weight in every relationship mechanic, they inform household alliances the same way any romantic bond does, and formal continuity of the line remains available through adoption, exactly as real history shows it working at the very top of Roman society.

Pregnancy carries genuine period-appropriate stakes by default — real risk to both mother and child at childbirth, moderated by health and medical care — with an accessibility toggle available at game start for players who'd rather dial that down without losing the system entirely. An affair discovered plays out through the same scheme-and-discovery mechanics used everywhere else in the game, scaling from a quiet, low-stakes embarrassment up to a genuine confrontation with real choices attached: forgiveness, divorce, or a formal challenge — with the period's own real (and harshly gendered) adultery law available as an extreme, rarely-exercised legal backdrop.

### Weddings — the Celebration Layer

It's worth being precise about where marriage itself ends and the wedding celebration begins, because *Gens* keeps them as genuinely separate systems. Everything above — dowry, consent, affectio maritalis, legitimacy — is Familia's own marriage math, and it's already complete the moment two people mean to be married. The **Wedding**, described in full in [Weddings](../design/gens-weddings-design.md), is the optional, real social event built on top of that decision: a betrothal (with its own ring exchange), the wedding day itself — a ceremonial hairstyle, taking the auspices, a public procession from the bride's family home to the groom's, carrying her over the threshold — and a closing feast, staged at a scale and guest list you choose. You can pick from three historical ceremony forms (the old-fashioned *confarreatio*, the more accessible *coemptio*, or simple cohabitation under *usus*), or skip the whole occasion through **elopement** — cheap and available to anyone, but forfeiting a proper wedding's prestige and relationship benefits, and a real, felt slight to any patron who expected to be involved.

## Growing Old

Reaching the Elderly stage isn't just a stat penalty — it's a real, playable phase of life, detailed in [Retirement & Old Age](../design/gens-retirement-old-age-design.md). Abilities and health drift downward gradually rather than falling off a cliff, and a long enough Elderly stretch can leave a lasting **Infirmity** — a standing penalty from the accumulated toll of age, like gout from a rich diet or failing eyesight (which the household answers honestly, with a reader rather than an anachronistic pair of glasses). Age also brings something real and positive: a character can become **Venerable**, carrying the genuine social authority Roman culture attached to old age — the Senate's own name comes directly from the word for "old man."

**Retirement** is a deliberate choice, available once a character reaches the Elderly stage, and it comes in two forms. **Full Retirement** hands off headship of the household entirely, just as if the head had died, except that they remain alive afterward as an **Elder Statesman** — a real advisor whose accumulated experience gives a mentorship bonus to their successor and whose advice genuinely improves the odds of a major decision. **Partial Retirement** steps back from one specific office or command without giving up headship at all. Nothing forces anyone to retire, either — a Proud or Power-Hungry head can cling to a post well past the point their declining abilities are actually hurting it, and a household can genuinely fail to ever talk them out of it.

## Succession & Dynasty

*Gens* promises "no forced ending" — the game never declares an artificial win or loss while there's still someone to play — but that's a promise about the game's own posture, not a guarantee that your line can never actually run out. The full mechanics live in [Succession & Dynasty](../design/gens-succession-dynasty-design.md).

At any time, the current head can set a quiet, reversible **preferred heir**, or make a **formal Declaration** — a public act that grants the heir a standing and loyalty boost and substantially reduces the odds of a contested succession, at the cost of a real penalty if reversed on a whim. A head can also formally **disown** an heir outright, a costly, public rupture rather than a quiet decision.

**Adoption** is a genuine political tool here, not a backup plan — the same evaluation used for a marriage candidate applies to a promising client, a rival house's spare son, or a distinguished freedman's child, and it's the honest mechanism behind stories like Augustus building his own succession. Passing over a blood heir in favor of an adopted one is a real slight, and a real source of the succession drama described next.

**Succession drama** — multiple ambitious heirs, a contested legitimacy claim, a blood heir resentful of an adopted rival — can escalate into a genuine contest: a legal challenge, a scheme against the chosen heir's standing, or in the harshest case open conflict within the family itself. A rival claimant who loses doesn't have to disappear — they can take a share of the inheritance and found a **splinter house**, the bitter mirror of an amicable branch split, and often the origin of a lasting grudge between the two houses afterward. A frequency toggle at game start lets you dial this drama up or down.

When an heir is still a child at the moment of succession, a **Regent** — typically the surviving spouse, who you actually play as during this stretch, or otherwise the household's senior appointee — runs the estate on their behalf until they come of age. And if a house genuinely runs out of every eligible heir, it can end — a rare, honestly-earned conclusion the Chronicle records in full, rather than a punitive game-over screen. Accessibility settings at game start can soften this from "realistic" down to a guaranteed safety net, or turn it off outright.

## Honoring the Dead

Your household's relationship with its own living gods — the Lares, the Penates, the head's own guardian spirit — belongs to religion, covered elsewhere. What belongs here is something related but genuinely distinct: the Roman dead themselves, and the ongoing cult a family keeps for them. Full detail is in [Ancestor Veneration & Funerary Customs](../design/gens-ancestor-veneration-funerary-customs-design.md).

Every death now passes through a real funeral sequence: the body is laid out, then a **Funeral Tier** — Modest, Proper, or Grand — is chosen, trading real treasury cost for lasting honor. A Grand funeral for a genuinely distinguished family is a real historical spectacle: actors wearing the household's ancestor masks and dressed in their remembered offices literally walk the procession, turning the funeral into a parade of the family's own history. Burial method — cremation or inhumation — mostly follows your household's culture and faith rather than being a free choice, drifting slowly with the times across the game's centuries. A period of **mourning** follows, with real restrictions on hosting feasts, attending games, or opening courtships, and breaking those restrictions early is a genuine scandal. A widow's own mourning period runs especially long before remarriage is considered acceptable.

The **di Manes** — the collective spirits of the family's own dead — are honored at the tomb rather than the household shrine, chiefly through *Parentalia*, a real nine-day February festival of tending ancestral graves, and *Lemuria*, a stranger, more unsettling May rite warding off the restless dead. Together, these feed a new tracked value called **Memoria** — sitting alongside your household's Dignitas (what the living think of you) and Favor (what the gods think of you) as a third axis: what your own dead think of you, and how much of their memory you've actually kept alive. A long, rich Chronicle gives a household real Memoria that a young or unremarkable family simply doesn't have, regardless of current wealth — and a household that neglects its dead can lose it just as surely. Delivering a **funeral oration** for a genuinely significant ancestor is itself a real political act, a chance for an ambitious heir to make their own first public impression.

## Names and Reputation

Beyond a Combo Title's shifting personality snapshot, a character can earn something permanent: a real, historically grounded **epithet** or **agnomen** — a name that sticks, becoming part of how they're formally known from then on. Full mechanics are in [Epithets, Nicknames & Titles](../design/gens-epithets-nicknames-titles-design.md).

Real Roman practice offers three kinds. A **conquest agnomen** — *Africanus*, *Germanicus*, *Britannicus* — is earned by leading or centrally credited with a decisive military campaign against a specific people or region. A **virtue agnomen** — *Pius*, *Felix*, *Magnus* — is granted, formally or informally, for some other real, specific achievement. And a **mocking nickname** can attach itself with no say from its bearer at all — the game's own namesake example being the emperor everyone remembers by a nickname meaning "little boot" rather than his actual given name. A formal grant carries real weight and is essentially the positive mirror of a formal public disgrace; a crowd-given nickname arises organically from fame or from a spreading scandal, and — just like a real scandal — a character can try to suppress or outlive an unwanted one using the same damage-control tools available against any other public disgrace, though a sufficiently notorious nickname can outlast every attempt to shed it. A personally-earned agnomen can even become a permanent, inherited part of the family name for every generation afterward, exactly as Scipio Africanus's own descendants kept using his title long after he'd earned it.

(Scandal itself — how a disgrace spreads, how severe it reads, and how a household manages the damage — belongs to a different part of the game, covering politics and society; the connection here is only that a mocking epithet is often scandal's own lasting residue.)

## Rival Ordinary Families

Not every family in your settlement gets the full character treatment — nor should it. Below the noble houses you compete with sits a much larger population of ordinary families, tracked only in aggregate by default. A small, curated sample of them are pulled out as real, named **Notable Households** — a head, a household composition, an occupation, a home — so your settlement reads as populated by real people rather than a spreadsheet, without the game needing to simulate every single potter's family individually. Full detail is in [Notable Households](../design/gens-notable-households-design.md).

A household earns its Notable status either by random sampling, or because it became genuinely relevant to you — it rents property from you, it's party to a court case, a disaster struck it directly. From there it ages, marries, and inherits on the same lifecycle logic as your own family, just without full individual stat blocks behind every member. A Notable Household can fade back into the aggregate population if it stops mattering — or, in the more interesting direction, rise. A family that accumulates enough real wealth can cross the threshold into becoming a genuine rival house of its own, exactly the "rising family" story the game's noble-house rivalry system was always describing without saying where those rising families actually came from.

## How You Look

Every character in *Gens* has a rendered portrait and a written description, assembled the same way a real Roman household's own appearance would be — from fixed genetics, chosen dress, and durable body marking. The presentation layer that pulls all of this together — a one-line summary, a paragraph, or a genuine multi-paragraph description on request — is covered in [The Paper Doll](../design/gens-paperdoll-appearance-design.md); it renders the data, but almost none of the underlying facts actually belong to it. Build, natural coloring, and inherited features come from Familia and from the Beauty and Physique trait spectrums; cultural origin comes from a character's own culture; and clothing and body marking are their own full systems, described below.

**Fashion & Dress**, detailed in [Fashion & Dress](../design/gens-fashion-dress-design.md), gives every character a **Wardrobe**: a single overall **Outfit Tier** running from Meager up through Modest, Respectable, Fine, and Opulent, plus a small set of named, status-gated **Garment Slots** on top — a citizen's toga, a senator's broad purple stripe, priestly vestments, military insignia. Wearing (or being caught wearing) a garment you're not entitled to is a real, consequential act, not flavor text: a non-citizen in a toga is an immediate scandal flag, and a household displaying Tyrian purple in violation of a sumptuary edict has committed a real, checkable offense. Dressing is mostly automatic — the game selects the right outfit for the occasion (Everyday, Formal, Ceremonial, Mourning, and so on) without you needing to remember — but you can always override it, and your authority over what someone wears scales with who they are: near-total over the enslaved and hired staff, but a genuine request rather than a command for a free spouse or adult child, one that can be refused or resented depending on their own personality. A household can also dress its staff in matching **Livery** for real prestige when hosting guests, and dress itself becomes a real tool of concealment — an escaping slave in stolen fine clothing, a spy dressed to blend into a foreign culture, or simply someone traveling incognito to avoid attracting bandits.

**Hair & Body Marking**, detailed in [Hair, Facial Hair & Body Marking](../design/gens-hair-body-marking-design.md), covers everything durable that a character carries on their own body rather than over it: hairstyle (with a real, dated catalog of fashions running the whole length of the game's timeline, plus distinct traditions for the game's many other cultures), facial hair, tattoos, scarification, piercings, and the rarer, harsher permanent marks — a criminal's forced brand, a scar from real injury. Most of this is chosen or simply lived into, but some of it is inflicted: forced head-shaving is a real, if temporary, punishment; a punitive tattoo or brand is permanent. On the gentler end, a tattoo can also be entirely personal and commemorative — marking a birth, a great achievement, or a devotion to a particular god, sitting alongside your household's own ancestor masks as one more real, physical record of a life actually lived.
